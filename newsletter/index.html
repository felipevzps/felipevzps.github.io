Pallatom2: Navigating the De Novo Protein Universe with Atom-to-Atom Modeling

Guan, J., Ma, R., +6 authors, Wu, W.

Abstract: Protein binder design requires precise coordination of molecular structure, binding geometry, and experimentally measurable performance. We introduce Pallatom2, an efficient, precise Atom-to-Atom generative model that jointly models protein sequence, backbone conformation, side-chain geometry, and target-interface contacts. A unified Atom14 representation and residue-guided sparse all-atom attention enable bidirectional information exchange between residue-level and atom-level representations, coordinating global structure with local atomic detail in a compact model of just 65.9 million parameters. Its conditioning system provides fine-grained control over binder-target engagement and global and local protein conformations, supporting diverse target-specific design requirements. In silico evaluation demonstrates broad generative capability across long-chain monomers, multi-domain proteins, and homomultimers, with monomer sampling extending to 1,600 residues. In binder design, Pallatom2 achieves state-of-the-art in silico screening performance across all nine targets with cross-method benchmark data under the reported protocols. These in silico results translate into substantial experimental success: biolayer interferometry identifies 112 active designs among 210 tested candidates (53.3%), with binders obtained for all ten experimentally evaluated targets. Six targets yield picomolar-scale apparent affinities, including K_D values of 3.96 pM for SC2RBD and 33.8 pM for PD-L1, while TNF reaches 1.166 nM. Beyond de novo discovery, starting-interface-guided BCMA design preserves association kinetics despite substantial sequence divergence; partial diffusion improves TNF affinity by up to 7.57-fold; and CDR-associated interface remodeling converts an inactive de novo nanobody into binders with apparent K_D values of 4.31 and 5.93 nM. Together, these results establish Pallatom2 as a versatile model for protein design, combining efficient atomic modeling, leading in silico screening performance, and experimentally demonstrated capabilities in high-affinity binder discovery, interface transfer, affinity maturation, and recovery of inactive designs.

Fish Metastasis Atlas for the Systematic Analysis of Organ-Specific Dissemination of Cancer

Mazloom-Farsibaf, H., Lekk, I., +2 authors, Danuser, G.

Abstract: Metastasis is a multifaceted process in which cancer cells must overcome diverse biological barriers to establish secondary tumors and drive disease progression. Different cancers exhibit highly non random, organ specific patterns of dissemination, known as organotropism, which arise from cancer cell plasticity and interactions with the host microenvironment. A major challenge in the field is the lack of approaches that capture cancer cell dissemination at the whole organism scale while maintaining physiological relevance. Here, we introduce an in vivo zebrafish model coupled with a data driven computational framework to systematically map and compare patterns of cancer dissemination. Using patient derived Ewing sarcoma cell lines, global analyses reveal pronounced heterogeneity across individual cell lines, which are further resolved by local metrics that capture region specific colonization frequency and cancer cell visit density. This combined global-local approach identifies recurrent spatial preferences across the fish body and uncovers correlated organ targeting, indicating coordinated and structured dissemination dependent on cancer cell line and disease stage. The framework is robust and adaptable to diverse imaging datasets, providing a scalable platform for studying cancer-microenvironment interactions and spatially organized metastatic behavior for various cancer types as well as genetic and drug perturbations, with potential relevance for personalized treatment strategies.

Coupling environmental biosensors to inverter circuits in soil bacteria

Rocha, C. K. d. S., Rico, J., Goni-Moreno, A.

Abstract: Genetic logic gates, such as inverting NOT functions, are routinely designed and optimised for a narrow set of laboratory-standard chemical inputs. This reliance limits their utility in real-world applications, where engineered chassis must navigate complex, niche-specific signals. Focusing on environmental synthetic biology, we engineered four biosensors responsive to ecologically relevant physical and chemical inputs (iron, oxygen, temperature, and light) and systematically coupled them to three repressor-based NOT logic gates (QacR, SrpR, and LitR) to generate 12 composite sensor-gate circuits. To evaluate cross-host robustness, all constructs were benchmarked in two non-model soil bacteria: Pseudomonas putida KT2440 and Pseudomonas protegens Pf-5. Our findings demonstrate that overall circuit performance is non-additive and cannot be predicted simply by summing individual sensor and gate dynamics. While standalone biosensors reliably detected their cognate inputs, concatenation across regulatory junctions introduced non-linearities, emergent dynamics, and host-dependent effects that caused several pairings to deviate unexpectedly from their predicted behaviour. Furthermore, host context directly governed logic execution: P. protegens Pf-5 exhibited superior overall circuit-processing capacity, whereas specific configurations, such as iron-responsive gates, performed better in P. putida KT2440. All 12 constructs are modularly integrated using the Standard European Vector Architecture (SEVA) format, providing a benchmarked toolkit to program complex information processing in real-world environments.

Sense-to-sense changes in bacterial genetic codes: Experimental validation by cell-free translation and native-host proteomics

Kim, P. H., McSweeney, M. A., +6 authors, Schoeniger, J. S.

Abstract: In the sixty years since the publication of the complete genetic code [1], many variants of the classical codon table [2] have been observed to occur naturally, including both stop codon and sense-to-sense reassignment, but in bacteria the only experimentally validated variants are stop-codon reassignments. Recently, computational analysis with Codetta predicted bacterial genetic codes containing sense-to-sense changes. Using an Escherichia coli cell-free protein synthesis (CFPS) system, we tested tRNAs predicted to reassign arginine codons to tryptophan. These candidate CGG-decoding tRNAs from bacteria phylogenetically distant from E. coli restored cyan fluorescence from an sfGFP Y66R reporter in a dose-dependent manner when added to CFPS extracts containing canonical tRNAArg(CCG). Reactions lacking the CGG-to-Trp candidate tRNA remained near background. Reassignment was also observed with firefly luciferase and NanoLuc reporters, in which Trp incorporation restored activity and solubility. Intact-protein mass spectrometry yielded masses consistent with Arg-to-Trp substitution. Deep data-independent acquisition (DIA) proteomics of Anaerococcus prevotii, a species for which Codetta predicts CGG to code for Trp, revealed six peptides containing Trp encoded at native CGG codons and no peptides containing CGG-encoded Arg, consistent with native CGG decoding as Trp in this organism. Together, these results provide direct functional support for predicted bacterial sense-to-sense genetic codes and establish a cell-free framework for discovering natural tRNAs that expand translational coding capacity.

Scalable Multi-omics Diagonal Integration from Self-Mapping

Guan, L., He, C.

Abstract: Diagonal integration seeks to align single-cell multi-omics datasets in which distinct modalities, such as RNA and protein, are measured in different cells. Unlike horizontal integration, these datasets lack direct cell--cell correspondences and complete feature mappings across modalities. Leading methods for weak-linkage integration often infer anchor cells between modalities by searching for cross-modal similarities through predefined feature links, then use these anchors to guide alignment. Their required matching or anchor refinement can become computationally burdensome at atlas scale. Here, we introduce a novel self-mapping technique, a mechanism that aligns modalities by mapping each computationally decoded cross-modality omics profile prediction to its own observed inputs. We implement this strategy in SCALEMAP, a scalable, modality-agnostic method for diagonal integration. Across transcriptomic, epigenomic and proteomic benchmarks, SCALEMAP matches or outperforms leading alternatives while requiring substantially less runtime and memory. In large-scale experiments, SCALEMAP integrates more than 1.5 million multi-omic profiles in less than 5 minutes with under 20 GB peak memory, which enables rapid synthesis of massive single-cell multi-omics resources into unified biological representations.

Multimodal protein representations across human brain cell types

Dong, A., Li, M. M., +12 authors, Zitnik, M.

Abstract: The central nervous system (CNS) comprises diverse cell types whose molecular context shapes the roles of the proteins they express. Computational models offer a scalable way to study the roles of proteins across human brain cell types, with protein language models and interactome- or single-cell-based models capturing complementary information encoded in protein sequences and cellular network contexts. Human brain tissue is hard to access, so brain cell types make up a small fraction of the atlases single-cell models train on and of the benchmarks used for their evaluation. To capture these complementary sources of information in the CNS, we introduce SEMPER, a multimodal representation learning model that aligns protein language model representations with protein-protein interaction networks from 442 cell types of the adult human brain, giving each protein a sequence-informed representation in every cell type in which its gene is expressed. We show that SEMPER representations predict cell type-specific protein complexes and predict genes essential to the survival of dopaminergic neurons, measured in a genome-wide CRISPR screen. In zero-shot retrieval, SEMPER recovers clinically pursued drug targets for Parkinson's disease, Alzheimer's disease, multiple sclerosis, Huntington's disease and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis at higher rates than cell type-specific models. To further improve target recovery, we combine SEMPER with a large language model that reasons over protein functional annotations. The combination recovers more targets, with SEMPER providing experimentally derived cellular context that complements literature-based reasoning of the large language model.

Self-organized criticality in a mammalian RNA-interference circuit

Pla-Mauri, J., Güell, M., +1 author, Conde-Pueyo, N.

Abstract: Living systems have been argued to operate near critical points, but their relevance at the cellular level is poorly understood. Critical states allow for a high sensitivity to external inputs and maximal information transfer and display fluctuations at all scales. Critical points are very special, and require the fine tuning of a control parameter, which is hard to reconcile with the robustness of living cells. Self-organized criticality (SOC) proposes that feedback, rather than tuning, brings a system to its critical point. Here we show that RNA interference, in which messenger RNAs compete for shared silencing machinery and so queue for service, together with negative feedback gives rise to SOC in mammalian cells. In a synthetic circuit, a protein repressor whose mRNA competes with the mRNA of a fluorescent reporter that it represses senses the congestion of the queue, closing the feedback loop. Theory and stochastic simulations show that the loop holds the queue near its critical point despite changes in its parameters, and that expression becomes intermittent, with scale-free bursts. Measurements in human cell lines show the predicted power-law distributions of single-cell fluorescence. RNA interference could thus support engineered SOC in animal cells and may also contribute to SOC in natural systems.

Associative Learning and Memory Erasure in a Synthetic Multicellular System

Pla-Mauri, J., Sole, R., Conde-Pueyo, N.

Abstract: Learning is a basic form of adaptation that organisms display from neural systems to single cells. In associative learning, the pairing of two stimuli lets one of them evoke the response to the other. Engineering this behavior in a living cell is difficult, because coincidence detection, memory, and conditional readout must be wired into a single regulatory network. Here we build a consortium of five E. coli strains that distributes the operations of associative learning and communicates through one diffusible quorum-sensing signal. The same signal acts as the memory trace. One strain detects the coincidence of the two inputs, a second maintains the memory through positive feedback in the shared medium, a third reads it out on the conditioned stimulus, a fourth reports the unconditioned response, and a fifth erases the memory on command. We show experimentally that the assembled consortium learns the association, since a stimulus that is silent before training evokes a strong response after pairing. Memory is retained through a medium exchange in which both stimuli are removed, and then the conditioned stimulus alone evokes the response. The reporter is not produced without its input. An erase command returns the response to the background. A population model of the consortium accounts for the sequence of responses and gives design rules for writing, retention, and erasure, and a trade-off in storing memory in a shared extracellular signal. Distributing the operations over strains makes each of them separately tunable and testable and provides a route to microbial communities that learn from their history.

An anaerobic cell-free protein synthesis formulation for the in vitro study of oxygen sensitive proteins

Arnold, J. D., Zhang, B., +1 author, Mills, C. E.

Abstract: Anaerobic microbes harbor unique enzymes and metabolic pathways with potential biotechnological applications, yet these microbes remain difficult to study and engineer. Research is limited by the labor-intensive workflows required to maintain anoxic environments and the lack of genetic tools available for non-model anaerobes. Here, we present an anaerobic cell-free protein synthesis (CFPS) platform for the in vitro study of oxygen-sensitive enzymes. This study establishes two methods for conducting anaerobic CFPS, achieving an anoxic environment by (1) sparging with inert gas or (2) lyophilizing to remove oxygenated water. We show the functionality of our anaerobic CFPS formulation by producing oxygen-independent fluorescent protein reporters and a [4Fe-4S] cluster pyruvate:ferredoxin oxidoreductase (PFOR) with a specific activity of 5.2 {+/-} 0.3 x 10-4 U/{micro}L cell-free extract. This highly modular platform will allow study of diverse oxygen-sensitive proteins and requires only minor modifications to commonly used CFPS workflows.

Synthetic Hibernation Suppresses Osteosarcoma Growth and Protects Normal Tissue from Radiation

Chowdhury, R., Piscitiello, E., +12 authors, Tinganelli, W.

Abstract: Natural hibernation can arrest tumor growth, but whether this effect can be reproduced through repeated pharmacologically induced synthetic torpor (ST) remains unclear. It is also unknown whether the radioprotection conferred by natural and synthetic torpor extends to tumors, potentially compromising radiotherapy. Here, five 5 AMP induced torpor bouts administered every other day, a protocol designed to mimic repeated hibernation cycles (synthetic hibernation), markedly restrained osteosarcoma growth in female C3H/HeNRj mice bearing syngeneic LM8 tumors. Interestingly, growth suppression persisted long after the final torpor bout. When 6 Gy total-body X-ray irradiation was delivered during the second bout, tumor growth control was comparable with irradiation under normothermia, whereas ovarian follicular atresia was lower and remained similar to non-irradiated controls. Together, these findings support synthetic hibernation as a strategy to restrain tumor growth and mitigate normal-tissue radiation injury without compromising tumor control.

A novel interaction between MNT and CCDC6: structural insights and distinct roles in cell migration and the DNA damage response

Junco, V., Cabezon, E., +10 authors, Garcia-Gutierrez, L.

Abstract: MNT is a transcription factor of the MYC-MAX-MXD network whose biological functions remain incompletely understood. Here, we identify CCDC6, a tumor suppressor involved in the DNA damage response (DDR), as a novel MNT-interacting protein. Co-immunoprecipitation and interaction mapping analyses identified a part of the CCDC6 coiled-coil domain and the MNT bHLH-LZ region as major determinants of binding. Structural modelling further identified MNT residues predicted to mediate CCDC6 binding that are also critical for MNT-MAX dimerization. Transcriptomic analyses revealed substantial overlap between the gene expression programs regulated by MNT and CCDC6. Loss of either protein promoted cell migration, whereas MNT and CCDC6 cooperated in the regulation of {gamma}H2AX signalling, cisplatin responses and CCDC6 localization during the DDR. Together, our findings uncover a previously unrecognized connection between CCDC6 and the MYC-MAX-MXD network through its interaction with MNT, revealing an additional layer of regulation within this transcriptional network.

Overcoming resistance to KRAS inhibition in KRAS/PIK3CA co-mutated colorectal cancer

Ferret, L., Montano, J. L., +7 authors, Gordan, J. D.

Abstract: Oncogenic alleles of KRAS drive colorectal cancers (CRC) growth and are associated with worse disease outcomes. Despite potent allele-specific and pan-RAS inhibitors targeting now in clinical test, CRC patients are not experiencing the same therapeutic benefit as patients with other cancers. PI3-Kinase (PI3K) is a direct RAS effector and is co-mutated in about 45% of CRC cases, raising the possibility that frequent oncogenic PI3K mutations contribute to the limited benefit of KRAS inhibition in CRC. Using isogenic colorectal cancer cells engineered to harbor either of the two most common activating PIK3CA mutations, we show that oncogenic PI3K promotes resistance to the pan-RAS inhibitor daraxonrasib (RMC-6236). Global phosphoproteomic profiling revealed that PIK3CA-mutant cells undergo a distinct adaptive response to RAS inhibition, characterized by sustained PI3K-AKT signaling and extensive remodeling of pathways controlling cell-cell adhesion and junction signaling, alongside suppression of apoptotic signaling. Combined RAS and PI3K inhibition overcame this resistance, suppressed signaling through both pathways and induced apoptosis in PIK3CA-mutant cells. Together, these findings identify oncogenic PIK3CA as a determinant of resistance to RAS inhibition in KRAS-mutant colorectal cancer and provide a mechanistic rationale for combined KRAS and PI3K inhibition in KRAS/PIK3CA co-mutant tumors.

Loss of CDKN2A remodels Golgi morphology to create a lipid peroxidation vulnerability

Amalric, A., Yang, B., +10 authors, Buj, R.

Abstract: Loss of the tumor suppressor CDKN2A/p16 occurs in ~50% of human cancers, yet no therapies specifically target tumors with complete loss or reduced CDKN2A/p16 expression (hereafter CDKN2A/p16-Low). Identifying selective vulnerabilities in CDKN2A/p16-Low tumors therefore represents a critical unmet need. Here, we identify altered Golgi morphology as a vulnerability in CDKN2A/p16-Low melanoma. A CRISPR knockout screen revealed that genes associated with Golgi membranes are essential for CDKN2A/p16-Low cell survival. CDKN2A/p16 knockdown in human and murine cancer cells was associated with striking Golgi compaction. CDKN2A/p16 knockdown sensitized cells to pharmacologic Golgi disruption via lipid peroxidation both in vitro and in vivo. Notably, reduced Retinoblastoma-associated protein (RB1) expression did not recapitulate the increased sensitivity to Golgi disruption, supporting a role for non-canonical p16 functions in this vulnerability. Together, these findings uncover a previously unrecognized link between CDKN2A/p16-Low status and Golgi remodeling, revealing a Golgi-associated metabolic vulnerability in CDKN2A/p16-Low melanomas.

Deep Learning Identifies BMI-Associated Morphologic Features in Breast Cancer Tissue

Heredia, E. A., Papasian, L., +18 authors, Matasci, N.

Abstract: Multiple epidemiological studies have established obesity as an adverse factor in breast cancer incidence, progression, and clinical outcomes. However, the biological mechanisms underlying these associations remain incompletely understood. In peritumoral adipose tissue, obesity is associated with chronic inflammation, macrophage recruitment, and broader alterations in the local microenvironment. Whether these obesity-associated changes are accompanied by distinct morphologic features within the tumor and non-adipose stromal compartments remains unclear. In this study, we investigated whether an AI-based pathology foundation model could support the development of machine learning algorithms capable of identifying obesity-associated morphologic patterns in histopathology images, with a particular focus on tumor and stromal regions. In a cohort of 195 patients with breast cancer, machine learning models using vector embeddings from non-adipose stromal regions classified high BMI ([≥]30 kg/m2) versus low BMI (<25 kg/m2) with an average AUC of 0.78 (95% CI: 0.73-0.83), consistently outperforming models based on tumor-only or combined tumor-stroma regions. These obesity-associated stromal signatures were observed across subgroups defined by hormone-receptor status and age, indicating that they are not restricted to specific clinical populations. Complementary embedding-based dissimilarity analysis further showed that higher BMI was associated with reduced inter-patient heterogeneity in both tumor and stromal tissues. Overall, these findings suggest that obesity is associated with distinct morphologic alterations in both tumor and non-adipose stromal tissues. Notably, the stromal compartment emerged as a particularly informative indicator of obesity-associated morphology and demonstrated reduced inter-patient heterogeneity among individuals with high BMI.

Light power density governs local and systemic responses to photodynamic therapy

Jiang, H., Kanao, E., +3 authors, Nomoto, T.

Abstract: Photodynamic therapy (PDT) is a promising remedy against tumor cells by light activation of a photosensitizer, and its systemic antitumor effects are commonly attributed to immunogenic cell death and the release of soluble damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). Here, we show that light power density (mW/cm2), which is often treated as a mere technical parameter, determines whether PDT suppresses distant, unirradiated tumors. At an identical light dose (J/cm2), PDT at a low power density sustained singlet oxygen (1O2) generation in tumors, preserved tumor perfusion, and suppressed both irradiated and unirradiated tumors. PDT at a high power density resulted in reduced 1O2 generation, reduced tumor perfusion, and increased programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression in unirradiated tumors without inhibiting their growth. Serum levels of soluble DAMPs did not differ between the two conditions, whereas circulating small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) carried distinct protein cargo. Power density is thus a controllable determinant of the systemic effects of PDT, and circulating sEVs are candidate mediators of these effects.

NPM1::ALK fusion-derived circular RNAs drive aggressiveness in ALK-positive anaplastic large cell lymphoma

BABIN, L., Colras, L., +4 authors, Meggetto, F.

Abstract: Circular RNAs (circRNAs) derived from the NPM1::ALK fusion have been identified in ALK-positive anaplastic large cell lymphoma (ALK+ ALCL), but their functional contribution to disease progression remains poorly understood. Divergent RT-PCR and long-read Nanopore sequencing identified eight NPM1::ALK-derived circRNA variants across ALK(+) ALCL cell-lines, a CRISPR/Cas9-engineered model, and a patient-derived xenograft. The abundance of f-circA23N2 and f-circA26N3, recurrently detected, steadily increased in crizotinib-resistant cell models. Both, highly stable, were predominantly cytoplasmic. Their overexpression in SU-DHL-1 did not affect NPM1::ALK expression, cell proliferation, or cell-cycle, in the absence or presence of crizotinib. circRNA overexpression delayed subcutaneous tumor-growth without altering response to crizotinib. Xenograft analysis disclosed altered immune response pathways and changes in inflammatory and myeloid-recruiting cytokines. RNA-seq deconvolution identified remodeling of the myeloid compartment. Intravenous f-circA23N2 and f-circA26N3 shortened survival with systemic lymphoma dissemination. NPM1::ALK-derived circRNAs appear to be non-cell-autonomous modulators of cytokine signaling and myeloid remodeling contributing to lymphoma dissemination.

The SOX9 C-terminus encodes a transferable cBAF-recruitment module for enhancer remodeling and cell-state control

Raghawan, A. K., Li, Z., +9 authors, Sethi, N. S.

Abstract: Transcription factors select genomic targets, but how they recruit chromatin remodelers to convert DNA occupancy into cell-state-specific accessibility remains unclear. Here, we identify an intrinsically disordered SOX9 C-terminal module that recruits SMARCB1-containing canonical BAF (cBAF) to stem-like enhancers that open during APC-mutant adenoma formation. Acute, reversible SOX9 degradation displaces cBAF from ~80% of co-bound enhancers, exceeding effects on pBAF and ncBAF, and collapses accessibility and stem-like identity; SOX9 re-expression restores these features. C-terminal truncation preserves DNA binding but disrupts cBAF engagement and chromatin opening. Enforced SMARCB1 docking to truncated SOX9 partially restores cBAF recruitment, accessibility, stem-like transcription, and organoid self-renewal, identifying remodeler recruitment as a limiting effector function. The isolated C-terminus supports higher-order cBAF assembly and, when fused to dCas9 as COMET, redirects endogenous cBAF to targeted loci to open chromatin. Disordered TF regions can therefore encode transferable instructions that deploy specific remodeling complexes to control cell state.

PIANO enables generative modeling of gene expression and scales multi-omics integration to 100 million cells.

Ning Wang, Christopher Cardenas, +13 authors, Fenna M Krienen

Abstract: Single-cell RNA technologies enable the routine acquisition of transcriptomic atlases. However, these molecular profiles are influenced by overlapping sources of variation. Since these covariates confound comparisons, data integration is the first step in most analyses. Three challenges remain: correcting strong batch effects, scaling to millions of cells, and modeling how covariates influence gene expression. To address these challenges, we developed PIANO, probabilistic inference autoencoder networks for multi-omics, a deep learning framework whose central feature is a generative model of gene expression data. Additionally, PIANO achieves robust integrations and trains 10× faster than previous methods. PIANO accurately integrates single-cell data across species and across single-cell and spatial transcriptomics modalities. As practical applications, PIANO models spatially resolved gene expression during Alzheimer's disease progression in human brains and integrates over 100 million cancer cells to model drug perturbations. In summary, PIANO's integration and generative modeling capabilities will empower novel insights for a wide range of future studies.
