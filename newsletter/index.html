PhageHost-GNN: novelty-aware and locally adaptable strain-level Klebsiella-phage prediction

Momanyi, B. M., Qin, K., +1 author, Fan, L.

Abstract: Interest in bacteriophages as alternative treatments for bacterial infections has grown amid the ongoing crisis of multidrug resistance. However, their narrow host range makes it labor-intensive to identify matching phages for bacterial isolates using conventional experimental methods. Although existing computational models based on biomolecular features have shown promise for phage-host matching, the performance of alternative model settings, including data embeddings, across datasets with different degrees of novelty remains underexplored. This study introduces PhageHost-GNN, a novelty-aware framework using routed models to predict strain-level Klebsiella-phage interactions, evaluated under three clinical scenarios. Using multiple embedding models to encode sequence, structural, and genomic-context features, the framework achieved AUPRCs reaching 0.784, 0.731, and 0.642 in host-unseen, phage-unseen, and both-unseen in silico evaluations, respectively. Benchmarks on experimentally validated datasets showed the clearest advantage over fixed embeddings when both hosts and phages were new to the trained model. In addition to direct inference and de novo training, the framework further implements a fine-tuning pipeline to adapt existing models to local custom datasets while reducing model-training time. Overall, PhageHost-GNN provides a flexible framework for model selection, evaluation, and local adaptation in practical phage-therapy screening.

An Expert-Curated Benchmark for Frontier and Local LLMs Querying a Biomedical Knowledge Graph

Medagbe, J. F. P., Kilyungi, B. M., +5 authors, Prins, P.

Abstract: We combine GeneNetwork's 1TB structured database with its metadata as an RDF graph and an associated literature corpus. We benchmark frontier and local LLMs querying it through four architectures: document, graph and agentic retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), and a combination of the three. We evaluate them on 40 domain specific expert-curated questions with a new metric: the satisfaction frequency psi (psi, i.e, the share of questions answered with precision and recall of at least 70% or an f1 of at least 60%). psi ranks systems the same way precision, recall and f1 -- a machine learning metric built from the harmonic mean of precision and recall -- but is easier to read and assess for humans. System architecture had a large effect: with full use of Claude Opus 4.8 the plain document RAG of our earlier GNQA system had a psi of 36% against 86% for our new combined tools. Delegating most of the work from Claude Opus 4.8 to a local model halved query costs while still reaching a psi of 59%.

Understanding when and how knowledge graphs add value to EHR-based clinical prediction across Parkinson's disease, multiple sclerosis, and chronic pain

Pathak, A., Stroganov, O., +1 author, Khasanova, T.

Abstract: Biomedical knowledge graphs (KGs) have emerged as a promising approach for incorporating biological knowledge into machine-learning models trained on electronic health record (EHR) data. However, their large scale and heterogeneous structure impose substantial computational cost, and it remains unclear whether their benefits generalize across diseases or depend on how the graph is constructed. We systematically evaluated KG integration across three diseases spanning distinct biological and clinical characteristics: multiple sclerosis, Parkinson's disease, and chronic pain. For each disease we compared five modelling strategies, spanning EHR-only features and graph diffusion over concept-level, cohort-derived, disease-anchored, and full SPOKE (Scalable Precision Medicine Open Knowledge Engine) subgraphs, asking whether graph construction affects predictive performance and computational cost, whether KG integration improves classification over EHR-only models, and what the graph does to model behavior. Cohort-derived subgraphs, comprising roughly 45% of SPOKE's nodes and one-fifth of its edges, matched or modestly exceeded the full graph's performance while substantially reducing computational cost in all three diseases. The predictive benefit of KG integration, however, was strongly disease-dependent: it improved classification in Parkinson's disease, had no measurable effect in multiple sclerosis, and reduced performance in chronic pain. These differences were not explained by the amount of biological information incorporated: the disease with the largest shift toward molecular features showed no improvement, the sole beneficiary showed only a modest shift, and the disease with no molecular shift was penalized. Mechanistically, graph diffusion redistributed each disease's existing signal across many correlated features rather than adding independent biological signal, so that any gain depended on whether it added information beyond the EHR. These findings show that cohort-derived subgraphs offer a computationally efficient alternative to full-scale knowledge graphs, and that KG integration functions less as a universal accuracy booster than as a data- and disease-dependent transformation of the patient representation.

Concurrent de novo inference of cell types and gene regulatory networks with NetMap

Hartebrodt, A., Oubounyt, M., de Almeida, G. P., +4 authors, Blumenthal, D. B.

Abstract: Existing methods for GRN inference based on single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) data typically require prior cell type calling and then infer GRNs at cell type resolution. This risks propagation of errors from cell annotation to GRN inference. Moreover, it makes existing methods blind to regulatory programs that are shared between different cell types, as well as to subtle differences in regulatory activity between cells of the same type. To address these limitations, we present NetMap, a pipeline that jointly infers cell-level GRNs and clusters cells based on shared regulatory programs, combining state-of-the-art single-cell autoencoders and explainable artificial intelligence methods. NetMap outperforms existing methods on synthetic data with a known ground-truth GRN and is able to reliably detect biologically plausible regulons on real data. Moreover, requiring only a small set of user-provided marker genes, NetMap can be used for GRN structure-informed probabilistic cell type assignment. We demonstrate the utility of this approach using PBMC data, where we achieve near perfect clustering and cell type recovery performance compared with manually created cell type labels. Finally, we applied NetMap to a data set with naive, activated and exhausted T-cells, and were able to verify that NetMap retrieves relevant T-cell state signatures from the data, with two approaches. NetMap can be installed as a python package from https://github.com/bionetslab/netmap and is compatible with the scverse system. Scripts to reproduce our results can be found at https://github.com/bionetslab/netmap-evaluation.

CHORUS: A proteome-scale atlas of membrane interaction interfaces constructed using cross-protein language model embeddings from sequences alone

Zhao, J., Huang, S., Sun, J.

Abstract: Membrane proteins drive vital cellular signaling and bioenergetics yet resolving their physical interaction interfaces remains hindered by the paucity of experimental complexes and the breakdown of sequence alignments across hydrophobic bilayers. Here, we present CHORUS, a sequence-only deep-learning framework that decodes interaction sites across membrane proteins without requiring 3D structures, multiple sequence alignments (MSAs), or predefined partner identities. By systematically synthesizing complementary latent representations across diverse protein language model (pLM) families and functional layer depths, CHORUS outperforms 18 established sequence- and structure-based methods across 4,655 homology-isolated test contexts (average precision: 0.643), demonstrating amplified predictive capacity within the lipid-embedded core (0.746). We show that CHORUS delineates quaternary contact boundaries across megadalton respiratory supercomplexes directly from sequences alone (0.984). We deliver the CHORUS Atlas (https://chorus.atlas.sunnylab.org), providing precomputed, residue-resolved interaction landscapes for 80,619 reviewed transmembrane proteins spanning 4,918 species and 33.7 million residues to empower interactome-scale discovery and allosteric therapeutic design.

Benchmarking interface confidence as a selection criterion for computational nanobody design against survivin's BIR-domain groove

Gately, C., Guan, X., Ding, X.

Abstract: Designed binders are routinely filtered on predicted interface confidence, which reports how self-consistently a model places an interface, not where it lies. We designed 200 nanobody loops against the survivin BIR-domain groove, co-folded 196, and checked epitope contact in every predicted complex. Confidence showed no detectable association with epitope engagement ({rho} = +0.100, 95% CI -0.04 to +0.24, n = 196); its highest and lowest quartiles failed alike (odds ratio 0.97, 95% CI 0.41 to 2.26). It tracked instead the run's other confidence estimates ({rho} = +0.61). Twelve-seed replication separated two candidates the criteria had not: one reached the complete epitope in 10 of 12 runs, the other never. Across 155 predictions the top-ranked model was no better than one drawn at random from its five (P = 0.610), though only 3 of 31 failed on every model. Nothing was assayed; nb_cand_057 is an in silico candidate only.

CRISCross: Integration of Epigenetic Information for Deep-Learning-Based Prediction of CRISPR-Cas9 Off-Targets Across Cell Types

Jobson Pargeter, W., Rabsch, D., +6 authors, Backofen, R.

Abstract: CRISPR-Cas9 enables precise genome editing but remains limited by off-target effects, raising safety concerns and necessitating accurate prediction. Although epigenetic features influence Cas9 binding and cleavage, their predictive signals are often weak, highlyvariable, and context-dependent, limiting the effectiveness of existing machine learning approaches. Cross-cell-type settings are especially challenging: epigenetic landscapes differ substantially, and models trained on one cell type frequently fail to generalize. Here, we systematically investigate the role of epigenetic context in off-target prediction and demonstrate that its utility depends on modeling interactions with sequence features rather than treating it as an independent signal. Tabular and deep learning experiments demonstrate that epigenetic features provide a consistent but modestly complementary signal, and that late-stage fusion strategies are insufficient to effectively exploit this information. To address this, we introduce CRISCross, a cross-attention-based architecture that captures sequence-epigenetic interactions at the nucleotide level, alongside CRISPRAT, a synthetic pretraining strategy that learns transferable representations from genome-derived sgRNA-off-target pairs without relying on dataset-specific pretraining. CRISCross, incorporating AlphaGenome-derived chromatin accessibility signals, shows better predictive performance than sequence-only baselines across three independent test datasets spanning two cell types, demonstrating improved generalization across guide RNAs, datasets, and cellular contexts. This is particularly relevant for therapeutic applications involving cell types or patient-derived contexts that lack dedicated experimental datasets. In such settings, predicted chromatin accessibility may provide scalable contextual information when direct epigenomic profiling is unavailable or impractical. Together, these results show that accurate CRISPR off-target prediction benefits from interaction-aware integration of epigenetic context alongside generalizable pretraining strategies.

DNA methylation-based prediction of differentiation state at cell-type resolution reveals an age-associated epigenetic mesenchymal drift

Teschendorff, A., Tong, H., Guo, X.

Abstract: Quantifying the differentiation state of cells is an important task in regenerative medicine, cellular reprogramming and in elucidating how differentiation hierarchies are altered in aging and disease. For DNA methylation data (DNAm) there is however an accute shortage of methods for quantifying differentiation state. Here we use machine learning to build EpiDiff, a predictor of differentiation that yields a continuous absolute differentiation index (DI) when applied to a DNAm sample. We extensively validate EpiDiff in single-cells, sorted cells and bulk tissue, as well as in in-vitro differentiation, organoid, reprogramming and cellular rejuvenation experiments. In mixed samples, EpiDiff measures the extent of differentiation across all cellular lineages. In sorted cell populations, we find that DI increases with age and, underpinning this increase, we discover that many cell-types undergo a developmental epigenetic drift that is most prominent towards the mesenchymal lineage. When applied to normal-tissues, EpiDiff is predictive of carcinogenic exposure and cancer risk. In summary, EpiDiff is a unique, general and versatile tool that can accurately quantify the global differentiation state of a DNAm sample with broad applicability to many disciplines.

Optimizing Therapeutic Protocols: Insights from Neural Network Analysis of Fractional-Order Tumor Models.

Khalid K Ali, Mohamed S Mohamed

Abstract: This paper describes a mathematical model of tumor growth during chemotherapy, employing a fractional calculus approach to account for the interactions between cancer cells, normal cells, immune cells, and drugs. This method better reflects the role of biological memory, or the state of a system at a given point in time. Using the analysis of neural networks to solve the mathematical equations, it is shown how the treatment regimen impacts the development of these cell levels over time. These simulations test the following biologically relevant parameters: the strength of the immune response, the effectiveness of the drugs, the rates of growth of cancer cells versus other cells, and the spatial geometry of the tumor micro-environment. The results show how cancer cell decline, immune system stimulation, and healthy cell repopulation interact in time. It is shown that by adjusting the parameters of the chemotherapy regimen, it is possible to optimize tumor reduction while maintaining healthy cell levels. This study suggests that complex calculus models of biological systems like cancer development can provide the means for more effective chemotherapy.
