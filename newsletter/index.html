Internet of Genetic Things (IoGT): Programmable Long-Distance Cybergenetic Logic Circuits Integrating IoT, Optogenetics, and Bacterial Gene Expression

Paul, A., Basu, R., +2 authors, Bagh, S.

Abstract: The Internet of Things (IoT) exchanges information and coordinates functions among geographically separated electronic devices. However, establishing analogous long-distance networks between genetically engineered living systems remains a challenge because synthetic genetic intercellular communication largely relies on local molecular signal transport. Here, we introduced a cybergenetic framework named the Internet of Genetic Things (IoGT) that autonomously regulated long-distance gene expression between geographically separated bacterial populations by integrating IoT, bacterial gene expression, and optogenetics. We engineered interconnected IoT nodes containing genetically engineered Escherichia coli, in which colour signals arising from reporter gene expression were detected using optical sensors interfaced with a Raspberry Pi. When gene expressions reached predefined thresholds and patterns, data were transmitted over the internet to a remote node, triggering blue light illumination to induce target gene expression in an optogenetically engineered E. coli population. Using this genetic, electronic, genetic communication architecture, we demonstrated cybergenetic BUFFER and OR gates between bacterial populations distributed across nodes separated by 100 m, as well as a cybergenetic AND gate coordinated among three locations. To demonstrate geographically extended operation, we constructed a cybergenetic NOT gate connecting a surface laboratory with a deep-subsurface laboratory located 555 m underground and more than 200 km away. To our knowledge, this was Indias first deep-subsurface biology experiment. IoGT was programmable. It reconfigured the same engineered bacterial populations into diverse Boolean logic gates BUFFER, OR, AND, and NOT gates. These results established IoGT as a programmable cybergenetic architecture capable of remote, multi-node intercellular gene expression control, having significant promise for distributed synthetic biology systems operating across remote or extreme environments.

Deep Learning Prediction of DNA Damage Response Protein Signaling from H&E Histopathology and Reverse-Phase Protein Arrays in Pan-Gynecologic and Breast Cancers

Lane, S. P., Zou, Y. S.

Abstract: Defects in homologous recombination and the DNA damage response (DDR) make gynecologic and breast tumors sensitive to platinum chemotherapy and PARP inhibitors, but identifying them requires costly genomic testing that reads permanent genomic scars rather than current pathway activity. Routine hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) slides are available for nearly every patient. This project asked whether H&E morphology carries information about functional DDR protein signaling across five cancer types in a single model. We matched 1,673 diagnostic whole-slide images from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) to Level 4 reverse-phase protein array (RPPA) data for 1,527 patients with breast (BRCA, n = 858), endometrial (UCEC, n = 403), cervical (CESC, n = 146), ovarian (OV, n = 72), and uterine carcinosarcoma (UCS, n = 48) tumors. Slides were tiled at 0.5 m/px and Macenko-normalized, yielding 20.4 million tiles. Tiles were embedded with two frozen pathology foundation models, UNI2-h and Virchow2. A gated attention-based multiple instance learning (ABMIL) network jointly regressed six RPPA pathway scores, including a DNA_damage composite of 11 DDR proteins. Performance was assessed by out-of-fold Spearman correlation under 5-fold, cancer-type-stratified, site-grouped cross-validation and compared against a baseline that predicts each patient's cancer-type mean. Predicted DNA_damage correlated with measured values ({rho} = 0.43 for both encoders) and exceeded the cancer-type baseline ({rho} = 0.31; {Delta} {rho} = +0.13). The signal held within cancer types (within-type {rho} = 0.29 for UNI2-h, 0.30 for Virchow2). With the original UNI encoder, it survived site-held-out folds with residualized targets (within-type {rho} = 0.26), and it vanished when targets were shuffled within cancer type (within-type {rho} [≤] 0.00 for all three encoders). All six pathway scores were predicted above baseline; Cell_cycle was strongest ({rho} {approx} 0.59). In interpretability analyses, the top 10 attention tiles per slide contained more tumor (+1.8 SD), larger tumor nuclei (+1.0 to +1.3 SD), and fewer lymphocytes (-0.6 SD) than the bottom 10 tiles; 13 of 14 morphometric features differed (q < 0.05). Attention agreed with Integrated Gradients (median {rho} {approx} 0.70). Routine H&E contains a moderate, reproducible signal of DDR protein abundance beyond cancer type, concentrated in tumor-dense regions with enlarged nuclei. Separating this signal from proliferation and predicting phosphorylated CHK1 and CHK2 directly are the next steps toward a functional, slide-based DDR biomarker.

PMRIPrep: automated preprocessing and quantification of DSC and DCE MRI

Rulseh, A. M., Rajan, A., +3 authors, Chawla, S.

Abstract: While dynamic susceptibility contrast (DSC) MRI measures hemodynamics and dynamic contrast-enhanced (DCE) MRI characterizes vascular permeability, quantitative values from both modalities remain processing-dependent. We present PMRIPrep, a Brain Imaging Data Structure (BIDS) App for automated DSC and DCE preprocessing and quantification. PMRIPrep adapts to available acquisitions and metadata, integrates both modalities within a session, and supports four DSC deconvolution methods and four DCE pharmacokinetic models. In DSC simulations with 4% cerebral blood volume and signal-to-noise ratio 50, pooled median absolute cerebral blood flow error was 13-19% across methods. Median Ktrans bias remained [≤]2% across four model-matched DCE configurations over 0-10% noise. End-to-end OSIPI analyses yielded DCE accuracy and clinical test-retest repeatability scores of 0.85 and 0.85, respectively. Outputs follow BIDS-Derivatives conventions and include machine-readable provenance and visual quality-control reports. PMRIPrep is freely available as a containerized application.

Archetypal analysis provides interpretable mixture representations of microbiome community structure

Verma, R., Albert, A., +1 author, Jha, A.

Abstract: Microbiome studies routinely reduce high-dimensional community profiles to a small number of coordinates for visualization and comparison. Ordination methods are powerful for detecting compositional differences, but their axes are often difficult to translate into explicit microbial community configurations; conversely, hard clustering can impose discrete states on variation that is frequently continuous. Here we evaluate Archetypal Analysis (AA) as an intermediate representation in which each microbiome is expressed as a convex combination of a small number of data-supported extreme community profiles. Across three applications, AA recovered biologically recognizable structure while preserving graded variation among samples. In a multi-biospecimen human microbiome dataset, different sample-source categories occupied characteristic regions of archetype space, while stool samples were represented by heterogeneous mixtures of archetypal profiles. In an early-life gut microbiome dataset, archetype usage changed systematically with age, describing microbial succession as changing contributions from community endpoints. In domestic dogs spanning forager, agrarian, pastoralist, urban, and industrialized lifestyles, groups showed characteristic but non-discrete combinations of archetypes, supporting generality across host species and ecological contexts. Across the displayed K-sensitivity analyses, the principal sample-source and early-life organization remained recognizable as model resolution changed, while repeated initialization and resampling analyses recovered similar archetypal structure. Held-out samples projected onto fixed training-derived archetypes retained metadata-associated organization within each dataset. Bray-Curtis principal coordinates analysis recovered the same broad biological structure, indicating that the value of AA is interpretive rather than simply greater separation, while taxa prioritized by AA retained substantial predictive information relative to equally sized random taxon sets. Together, these results support AA as a complementary microbiome representation that connects low-dimensional sample coordinates directly to interpretable taxonomic profiles and provides a natural framework for describing ecological states, gradients, and transitions without hard cluster assignments.

mRNAspect: Real-Time Quality Control of Therapeutic mRNA with N1-Methylpseudouridine Bias Correction Using Nanopore Direct RNA Sequencing

Lee, S.

Abstract: Quality attributes of therapeutic mRNA include sequence identity, transcript completeness, and poly(A) tail length. While these attributes require monitoring from development through manufacturing, each needs a separate assay. Nanopore direct RNA sequencing enables rapid quality control because it measures all three on the same molecules within a single run, and the resulting data are available during sequencing. However, N1-methylpseudouridine (m1{Psi}), which often replaces uridine in therapeutic mRNAs, alters the nanopore signal, biasing basecalling and poly(A) estimation. We present mRNAspect, a pipeline combining real-time analysis and m1{Psi} bias correction. mRNAspect includes a fine-tuned basecaller and a poly(A) correction based on the reference sequence. mRNAspect is a packaged macOS app that ingests POD5 or BAM files by watching the MinKNOW output directory as sequencing proceeds. Its graphical user interface shows the latest snapshot of the results, and it writes HTML reports as each input file is processed and a PDF report when the run is finished. When measured on Linux with a single NVIDIA RTX 2080 Ti, each 3-min batch of sequencing (about 5,700 reads) was processed in 38 s. The basecaller was fine-tuned on in vitro transcribed (IVT) constructs and cDNA-IVT libraries. Across 10 m1{Psi} constructs held out from training, the fine-tuned basecaller reduced the error rate from 20.1% to 7.2%, compared with 3.0% for unmodified RNA. A poly(A) correction was fitted to constructs with both modified and unmodified pairs. The correction accounts for the fraction of uridine in the reference sequence and the number of uridines within the 12-nt sequence adjacent to the tail. Across eight constructs, the correction reduced the poly(A) tail length difference between modified and unmodified pairs from up to 9.0 nt to within 1.8 nt.

Designing a Saccharomyces cerevisiae Cell Factory for the Overaccumulation of Tyrosol using Rational Design and Genome-Scale Metabolic Modeling Approaches

Guillen Gonzalez, A., Moreno Avitia, F., +2 authors, Guillamon, J. M.

Abstract: Tyrosol (TyOH) is a phenolic compound with antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, naturally present in two Mediterranean products, wine and virgin olive oil, and is the key precursor of Hydroxytyrosol or Salidroside. Its industrial production still relies on chemical synthesis, which conflicts with the EU Green Deal roadmap to transition to a clean, circular, resource-efficient economy by 2050. Thus, moving to a sustainable economy is a priority. Here, we engineered Saccharomyces cerevisiae as a cell factory to produce TyOH. To support this goal, we used ecFactory, a pipeline leveraging enzyme-constrained genome-scale metabolic models (ecGEMs), to predict gene targets, narrowing the candidate list and simplifying experimental validation for strain development. The modifications were tested in a medium composed mainly of glucose with ammonium sulfate as the sole nitrogen source. The final strain reached 876.91 {+/-} 20.37 mg/L in a 0.5 L bioreactor, demonstrating the potential of model-guided engineering for sustainable TyOH production.

Beyond canonical: Hidden phosphoregulatory control in RAS signaling reveals context-dependent rewiring in Y1 tumor cells

Montoni, F., Hanada, T., +6 authors, Natsume-Kitatani, Y.

Abstract: Cell signaling pathways are not static. Regulatory mechanisms finely control the signal across complex networks to promote signal propagation. Among them, phosphorylation is one of the most important regulatory mechanisms, in which signal cascades propagate signals to the nucleus, enabling responses. Phosphorylation events can be regulated by guanine nucleotide exchange factors (GEFs) and GTPase-activating proteins (GAPs) to coordinate the activation state of small G-proteins. In the Y1 cell derived from the adrenal gland of a mouse, the tumor phenotype is driven by constitutive overexpression of non-mutated KRAS with high SOS (Son of Sevenless)-mediated KRAS-GTP levels, activating downstream ERK signaling. Our previous finding showed that SOS is not a sole GEF in KRAS activation, pointing that much of the GEF and GAP regulatory system remains unknown. This motivated the generation of a comprehensive time-resolved proteome (DIA) and TMT phosphoproteome (DDA) under FGF2 stimulation combined or not with the SOS inhibitor BI-3406 (SOSi) to investigate adaptive signaling dynamics. Our findings revealed ERK feedback relief through parallel pathway activation and differential phosphorylation dynamics consequent of SOS inhibition, revealing novel layers of phospho-signaling rewiring beyond canonical RAS activation.

Targeting ATR-CHK1-WEE1 signaling enhances IMPDH inhibitor efficacy in mTORC1-driven tumor cells

Akther, M., Grumet, A., +5 authors, Valvezan, A. J.

Abstract: Uncontrolled activation of the master metabolic regulator Mechanistic Target of Rapamycin Complex 1 (mTORC1) in tumors confers strong dependence on the guanylate nucleotide synthesis enzyme inosine-5'-monophosphate dehydrogenase (IMPDH) for tumor cell growth and viability. Clinically approved IMPDH inhibitors reduce the guanylate nucleotide pool available for DNA synthesis in mTORC1-driven tumor cells, resulting in prolonged DNA replication stress and accumulation of DNA damage that culminates in apoptosis. These early molecular events are accompanied by activation of adaptive DNA replication stress response (RSR) and DNA damage response (DDR) pathways. We hypothesized that targeting these adaptive responses could enhance IMPDH inhibitor efficacy. We find that combining IMPDH inhibition with inhibition of the ATR-CHK1-WEE1 signaling axis increases the efficacy and durability of response in cell models, and in a syngeneic xenograft tumor model of the mTORC1-driven genetic tumor syndrome Tuberous Sclerosis Complex. Mechanistically, abrogating ATR-CHK1-WEE1 signaling during prolonged IMPDH inhibition causes aberrant cell cycle progression, increased DNA double-strand breaks, and formation of micronuclei. Together these findings uncover adaptive mechanisms that restrict IMPDH inhibitor efficacy, informing a combination strategy to improve treatment efficacy, while maintaining selectivity for targeting tumor cells that are driven by uncontrolled mTORC1 activation.

Fishing for new immunotherapy compounds to boost innate tumor rejection

Machado, A. B., Labao-Almeida, C., +12 authors, Fior, R.

Abstract: Despite significant advances in cancer immunotherapy, tackling immunosuppression remains critical to achieve more effective responses. While developing the zebrafish xenograft model, we found that some human tumors engraft very efficiently ("progressors"), while others are cleared by the host ("regressors"). We showed that this was dependent on innate immunity, which recognizes and eliminates some cancer cells, while others are able to evade and suppress it. This opened an opportunity to perform an in vivo phenotypic drug screen to find compounds that induce clearance by boosting innate-tumor rejection. Zebrafish xenografts of human colorectal (CRC) and breast cancer (BC) that show a high engraftment/low clearance phenotype were used to screen over 500 FDA-approved small-molecule compounds, with tumor clearance serving as the phenotypic readout. The screen identified 23 hit compounds, of which six were effective across both cancer models. Mechanism-of-action enrichment analysis revealed a significant overrepresentation of autonomic nervous system-targeting drugs among the hits, particularly cholinergic ones. Immune profiling in myeloid reporter lines showed that the lead hit, Tiotropium bromide, a muscarinic receptor antagonist, reprograms tumor-associated macrophages toward a pro-inflammatory, anti-tumoral state. Tiotropium significantly increased macrophage phagocytosis, and macrophage depletion completely abolished its pro-clearance activity, establishing these myeloid cells as essential effectors. In a syngeneic CT26 colorectal mouse model, Tiotropium synergized with anti-PD-1 to control tumor growth and improve survival, outperforming all monotherapy arms. This effect was immune-dependent, as it was lost in immunodeficient NSG mice. Together, these findings demonstrate that in vivo phenotypic screening of FDA-approved compounds in zebrafish xenografts is a productive strategy for innate immune drug discovery in cancer. The identification of Tiotropium as a myeloid-reprogramming innate immune primer, together with the unbiased recovery of the neuro-immune axis as a druggable target, opens new avenues for overcoming immunotherapy resistance.

BRIDGE: Backbone Representation for Inverse-folding-Driven Generalizable RNA Encoding

Song, J., Ko, J., +1 author, Shin, W.-H.

Abstract: RNA recognizes small molecules and proteins through their three dimensional structures, but sparse functional annotations limit task specific learning of transferable structural features. We present BRIDGE, an inverse-folding encoder pretrained on 2,501 RNA chain records from experimental structures. Each nucleotide is represented by its phosphorus atom and five ribose atoms (P, C1', C2', C3', C4', O4'). Base coordinates are not encoder inputs. From these atoms, the encoder is trained to predict nucleotide identity, solvent accessible surface area (SASA) and base geometry. With matched readouts, frozen BRIDGE representations reach a three-model ensemble pooled average precision (AP) of 0.627 for external ligand-binding site prediction and 0.647 for RNA-protein interface prediction, compared with 0.503 and 0.516 for frozen, randomly initialized encoders. Under the evaluated full-adaptation recipes, pretrained initialization reaches AP 0.703 for ligand binding and 0.641 for protein interfaces, versus 0.635 and 0.623 for training from scratch. Sequence-only pretraining, without SASA or base-geometry targets, also transfers to both tasks. Whether the base-geometry objectives add further value depends on the task and adaptation mode. In decoy ranking and apo/holo evaluations, transfer depends on the readout and the test set, and small target panels and downstream training overlap in the apo/holo evaluation limit broader conclusions. These results support inverse-folding pretraining as a route to transferable RNA structural representations that do not require observed base coordinates at inference.

Genome-guided vs transcriptome-guided mapping: systematic evaluation of RNA-seq quantification bias for tandemly duplicated genes in model and crop plants

Jing, Y., Wang, F., +2 authors, Wang, P.

Abstract: Accurate quantification of RNA-seq data is fundamental to modern plant biology. The choice of mapping strategy and aligner critically determines expression quantification accuracy and shapes the quality of downstream biological analyses. However, a pervasive yet often underappreciated challenge is the accurate quantification of genes located within tandemly duplicated arrays. Here, we selected six representative plant species (Arabidopsis thaliana and five important crops), and compared RNA-seq analysis results generated from two mapping strategies (genome-guided and transcriptome-guided) using three widely used aligners (TopHat2, HISAT2, and STAR). Our results reveal pervasive alignment errors in genome-guided strategies, arising primarily from misaligned reads spanning tandem gene duplicates, a gene class that plays important roles in plant defense and stress responses, detoxification, and secondary metabolism. TopHat2 with the transcriptome-guided strategy yielded the most accurate alignments, whereas transcriptome-guided HISAT2 and STAR corrected most, but not all, of the errors. Transcriptome-guided mapping also demonstrates superior performance over the genome-guided approach on simulated RNA-seq benchmark datasets. Moreover, in functional enrichment analysis, the transcriptome-guided strategy outperformed the genome-guided one in retrieving gene sets biologically relevant to the processes under study. Overall, this study provides a systematic evaluation of how mapping strategies and software selections affect RNA-seq quantification for tandem gene duplicates, and offers a valuable reference for standardizing plant RNA-seq analytical workflows, particularly for studies investigating biology processes regulated by tandem gene duplicates.

A noise-corrected metric for evaluating spatial transcriptomics predicted from histology

Ouyang, J., Gao, Y., +6 authors, Chen, H.

Abstract: Deep learning methods that predict spatial transcriptomics from histology offer scalable molecular profiling of clinical cohorts. However, conventional correlation metrics overstate prediction quality across whole sections by crediting models for recognizing broad tissue regions, and understate fine-scale prediction quality because sampling noise dominates sparse RNA counts. To resolve this, we introduce the reproducible-signal correlation (RSC) metric. RSC randomly splits RNA counts into two halves to separate reproducible signal from sampling noise across spatial scales and within tissue regions or cell classes. Across 12 spot-level and three single-cell methods, RSC revealed that most models capture broad tissue patterns but largely miss differences between neighboring spots or cells, which whole-section correlation overlooks. Within malignant cells, RSC distinguished methods that genuinely capture part of the cell-to-cell heterogeneity from those whose scores across all cells were inflated by cell-type composition. Guided by RSC, we designed a pairwise difference loss between neighboring spots that improved fine-scale predictions on average across 11 models. RSC thus provides a foundation for benchmarking predictions, guiding model design and determining which spatial analyses histology can support.

VenusPheno: Transfer-Guided Task Grouping for Multi-Task Bacterial Phenotype Prediction

Yuan, J., Zhou, B., Hong, L.

Abstract: Understanding how genomic information gives rise to bacterial phenotypes is a fundamental problem in biology. Genome-level prediction requires models that integrate information from thousands of proteins, while related phenotypes may share genomic signals. However, not all phenotype tasks benefit from being learned together, and suitable task groups can be difficult to define from biological knowledge alone. We present VenusPheno, a framework that combines multiple-instance learning over pretrained ProtT5 protein embeddings with transfer-guided multi-task learning. An attention-based Set Transformer aggregates the proteins in each genome into a genome-level representation. We evaluate VenusPheno on 14 binary phenotype tasks from 12,178 bacterial genomes using genus-disjoint splits. Under single-task training, VenusPheno outperforms computational, conventional machine-learning, and deep-learning baselines in terms of mean balanced accuracy. We then use leave-one-task-out analysis to estimate directed transfer relationship between phenotypes, revealing both beneficial and detrimental interactions. Grouping tasks based on these transfer relationships further improves mean balanced accuracy over independent training and joint training of all tasks. Although inferred without biological priors, the resulting groups are consistent with biologically plausible relationships among phenotypes. VenusPheno therefore combines accurate phenotype prediction with a data-driven strategy for determining which tasks should be learned together. The PyTorch implementation is publicly available at https://github.com/YJinling/VenusPheno.

Development of a Novel Multitarget Directed Ligand (MTDL), LCI132 for the Polypharmaceutical Treatment of High-Risk MYCN Amplified Neuroblastoma

Pal, D., Maddeboina, K., +6 authors, Durden, D. L.

Abstract: MYCN-amplified neuroblastoma (MNA-NB) is associated with poor prognosis due to the development of resistance to current aggressive therapy and to the subsequent limited response to single agent targeted therapeutics in the relapse setting. Herein, we describe the in silico design and development of the first-in-class nanomolar potent, selective, multitarget directed ligand (MTDL) against PI3K, BRD4, CDK4/6/9 and Aurora kinases A/B, LCI132, for the polypharmaceutical treatment of MNA-NB. LCI132 demonstrates a favorable safety and pharmacokinetics profile, suppresses viability, induces apoptosis in MNA-NB and inhibits in vivo tumor growth with no toxicity. Mechanistically our data show that LCI132 orthogonally inhibits MYCN transcription and induces proteasome mediated MYCN degradation. We and others recently observed an augmented cytostatic effect of combining CDK4/6 inhibitors with retinoic acid in MNA-NB models and the associated disruption of core regulatory circuits. Importantly, this was not associated with the induction of apoptosis. The combined treatment of MNA-NB cells with retinoic acid and LCI132 synergistically inhibited cellular proliferation and induced apoptosis in MNA-NB cell lines and ex vivo patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models. Combination of LCI132 and ATRA treatment effectively reduces levels of MYCN and alters the expression of CRC transcription factors. Collectively, our study provides mechanistic insights and a rationale for the combined use of ATRA and a MTDL, LCI132 as a novel polypharmaceutical therapeutic strategy for the treatment of MNA-NB NB.

The PD-L1 metabolic interactome in glioma is bounded by IDH1 genotype

Carretero-Navarro, P., Lopez-Larrubia, P., Pacheco-Torres, J.

Abstract: Immune checkpoint blockade has failed in glioblastoma, and part of the reason may lie in the tumour cell itself: if the checkpoint and the metabolism that sustains the cell hold each other up, then lowering one arm hands the tumour the other, and a single agent cannot win. We tested that possibility in GL261 glioma cells of IDH1-wildtype and IDH1-mutant genotype, silencing Chka or Cd274, blocking PD-L1 with an antibody, and reading the consequences by RT-qPCR, RNA sequencing, flow cytometry of surface PD-L1 and high-resolution 1H NMR of dual-phase cell extracts. The two genes proved reciprocal in both genotypes: silencing either transcript raised the other, and silencing choline kinase roughly doubled PD-L1 at the cell surface. Antibody blockade did something the silencing did not. In IDH1-wildtype cells it lowered soluble phosphocholine, the only one of twenty-two aqueous signals to survive correction for multiple testing, and raised total lipid per cell without changing lipid composition, whereas silencing PD-L1 raised phosphocholine instead; in IDH1-mutant cells neither change occurred. The transcriptome drew the same boundary, engaging a sterol programme and lowering glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid cycle and oxidative phosphorylation in wild-type cells only. The interferon response did not respect that boundary and rose under every perturbation in both genotypes. Removing PD-L1 from inside the cell and blocking it from outside are therefore not the same intervention, and the metabolic half of the response stops at the IDH1 genotype.
