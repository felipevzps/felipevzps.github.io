RADSuite: Improving Species-Level Identification in Microbiome Research

Christensen, R. N., Gilliland, M., +5 authors, Johnson, W. E.

Abstract: In the last twenty years, the microbiome has become a central focus of biomedical research, with amplicon sequencing of the 16S rRNA gene considered the industry standard for taxon identification of prokaryotes due to its cost-effectiveness. However, common target regions in 16S amplicons often do not uniquely distinguish closely related species, restricting many analyses to the genus level. Additionally, curated databases such as NCBI's 16S curated database often do not contain all intragenomic variants of the 16S gene and lack annotation of the 16S genomic composition and gene structure. To address these limitations, our team created RADSuite, a collection of tools which includes a more robust database of 16S gene copies (RADlib), an application that identifies which V regions will allow for species level identification (RADx), and a step-by-step guide for how to integrate RADlib within existing metagenomic profiling software tools. Here we demonstrate the utility of using RADSuite as a guide for designing 16S amplicon sequencing experiments. For example, RADx can be used to recommend 16S components that enable species-level resolution for most microbes of interest, even when industry-standard default amplicon choices (V3/V4) are insufficient. These findings suggest that targeted variable region selection is critical for reliable species identification. As researchers use RADSuite to improve the quality of their experiments, we anticipate improved experimental accuracy in fields of microbiome research from human health to environmental conservation.

MicroBioAgent: an AI agent for automated microbiome analysis

Li, Z., WANG, J., +2 authors, Zhang, L.

Abstract: Microbiome data analysis demands customized workflows tailored to specific biological questions, sequencing modalities, and data properties. However, constructing and executing these workflows requires navigating heterogeneous software dependencies, reference databases, and multi-step parameter tuning. Here, we present MicroBioAgent, an autonomous, domain-specific agent framework that translates high-level biological queries into executable, study-specific pipelines across 16S rRNA amplicon, reference-based shotgun, and de novo assembly-based metagenomics. Rather than relying on static pipeline templates, MicroBioAgent employs a closed-loop execution architecture: it dynamically selects and runs bioinformatics tools within user-controlled environments while iteratively inspecting intermediate outputs to adapt downstream analytical decisions. Furthermore, to address the long-running nature of metagenomic computation, we introduce an asynchronous background task-management mechanism that reduces LLM token consumption by over 50% while enabling real-time plan adjustment and automated workflow resumption. We systematically evaluated MicroBioAgent across three benchmarks of increasing complexity: (1) paired amplicon and shotgun profiling of tomato rhizosphere microbiomes, (2) taxonomic and functional profiling coupled with machine-learning classification in a colorectal cancer cohort, and (3) de novo metagenomic assembly, metagenome-assembled genome (MAG) binning, and metabolic pathway reconstruction in a polyethylene degradation study. Across all benchmarks, MicroBioAgent faithfully recapitulated the primary biological findings of the original studies, exhibiting high analytical concordance despite expected tool- and database-dependent variations. In systematic robustness evaluations across 27 independent runs spanning three backbone large language models and three prompt-complexity tiers, MicroBioAgent achieved a 98.8% task completion rate and yielded highly reproducible diversity and differential-abundance estimates. MicroBioAgent establishes a scalable, adaptive paradigm for end-to-end computational microbiome research.

Targeting protein degradation strategies in drug discovery: integrating PROTACs, ubiquitination biology, and AI-driven design.

Sanjeevi Pandiyan, Chandra Sekhar Bhol, +1 author, Gautam Sethi

Abstract: Targeted protein degradation (TPD) has emerged as a transformative therapeutic strategy for eliminating disease-associated proteins, including targets that remain challenging to modulate using conventional occupancy-driven inhibitors. Among TPD approaches, proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) exploit the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) to induce selective and event-driven degradation of proteins of interest (POIs). Despite rapid advances in PROTAC development, successful translation depends on interconnected determinants, including target degradability, ubiquitin ligase (E3) recruitment, warhead and linker properties, ternary-complex formation, ubiquitination efficiency, intracellular exposure, and pharmacokinetic behavior. This review provides an integrated, mechanism-oriented analysis of these determinants by linking PROTAC design, ubiquitination biology, computational approaches, and clinical development. We examine key principles governing PROTAC-mediated degradation, with emphasis on E3-ligase selection, linker design, ternary-complex geometry and cooperativity, ubiquitin (Ub)-acceptor lysine (Lys) accessibility, ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme (E2)/E3 context, proteasomal engagement, inefficient degradation, and the hook effect. Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning for degradation-activity prediction, E3-ligase/POI pairing, ligand and linker optimization, ternary-complex modeling, and absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET) assessment are critically evaluated alongside their technical and translational limitations. Preclinical developments, clinical-stage degraders, emerging TPD modalities, and major barriers to translation are also discussed. We propose an integrated framework linking distinct target ubiquitination patterns, degrader design, ternary-complex geometry, AI-enabled prediction, and pharmacokinetic/clinical constraints, providing a mechanism-informed roadmap for the rational development of next-generation protein degraders and identifying mechanistic bottlenecks and translational priorities across therapeutic settings.
