Minimal, synthetic arginine deiminase pathway provides full ATP demand for cellular growth

Schann, K., Houkes, J., +4 authors, Claassens, N. J.

Abstract: Bottom-up construction of synthetic cells requires a simple, yet robust ATP-generating system. The arginine deiminase (ADI) pathway offers a minimal, enzyme-based alternative to more complex ATP-producing systems such as glycolytic routes, respiratory chains or photosystems. Here, we implement the ADI pathway in Escherichia coli and test its capacity to fully replace native ATP generation. First, we designed and tested synthetic ADI operons through combinatorial library screening and selected high-performing variants. Using the best performing-ADI variant, we progressively reduced the cells dependence on native ATP generation while increasing selection for ADI-dependent growth through targeted knockout strains and growth conditions, followed by adaptive laboratory evolution. This resulted in a strain in which the ADI pathway sustained growth in minimal medium at a doubling time of 21 hours, serving as the sole source of ATP. Whole-genome sequencing of evolved strains revealed that this transition required loss-of-function mutations in the native respiratory chain. These findings demonstrate that the ATP supply of a cell can be fully supported by the ADI pathway, which has not been demonstrated in nature. Moreover, the ability of the pathway to sustain growth with a relatively high growth rate highlights its potential to fully power synthetic cells and support other biotechnological applications.

Genomic tessellations

Ekim, B., Cheng, H.

Abstract: Periodicity is ubiquitous in genomic data. Assembled sequences are dense with homopolymers, microsatellites, and large satellite arrays, which contribute to phenotype, variation, and disease. Third-generation sequencing technologies, such as Oxford Nanopore (ONT) and PacBio HiFi, has enabled longer and increasingly accurate reads, but have brought with them systematic error biases in repetitive regions, where insertions and deletions can distort array lengths and mimic or obscure biological variation. This periodicity is not benign: local repetitiveness makes sequence alignment ambiguous and genome assembly error-prone. In sequence analysis, these two manifestations of periodicity are simultaneously at play, each compounding the challenges of the other; a unified representation of repetitive and non-repetitive sequence is necessary to account for their combined effects consistently. We propose a paradigm under which (either repetitive and non-repetitive) DNA is split into its identity (its irreducible components) and its geometry (how its components are realized). A genomic tessellation {tau} is a canonical factorization of any sequence S of length n over the DNA alphabet into a sequence of tiles partitioning S, where each t in {tau}S is described as a quasi-periodic run of a canonical unit identity ut with geometry consisting of span st, phase {varphi}t, and a set of exceptions Xt. We show that, for a prescribed error bound k [≥] 1 and fixed maximum unit length P, S can be tessellated in O(n) time such that every tile has a unique identity whose periodic expansion differs from the tiled sequence by at most k edits, and that tiles sharing an identity are related by a bounded number of geometric adjustments. We introduce the tessellated Burrows-Wheeler transform (tBWT), which indexes {tau}S by concatenating, over all tiles in order, the identity ut of each tile. For a tessellated query q of m tiles, the tBWT counts the repeat-agnostic occurrences of q in O(m) time, and for sampling interval r locates its h occurrences in {tau}S in O(m + hr) time. We demonstrate that tessellations avoid the well-known speed-accuracy tradeoff and outperform state-of-the-art long-read mappers in repeat-dense regions in both speed and accuracy. On a 20x simulated set of both ONT and HiFi reads on the HG002 maternal X chromosome with synthetically-expanded repeat arrays, locating and chaining exact tile identity matches obtained from the tBWT is up to 57x and 83x faster, respectively, than the state-of-the-art mappers minimap2 and Winnowmap2, with comparable accuracy. Furthermore, by abstracting away differences in geometry, tessellations of a set of input sequences onto itself (an autotessellation) reveals homology otherwise obscured by array-length variation and local sequence divergence. A geometry-only polishing of an an ONT-only assembly of the HG002 maternal X chromosome with [~]50x PacBio HiFi reads improves assembly QV from 45.4 to 60.6 in under a minute using less than 10GB of memory. Tessellated-read evidence over the autotessellation of this assembly organizes 167 mitochondrial contigs totaling [~]4 Mbp into a single family with a [~]16.5kbp representative, which aligns to the HG002 Q100 mitochondrial genome with 100% identity. Genomic tessellations provide a canonical representation of repetitive and non-repetitive DNA and enable efficient sequence analyses while preserving homology.

Expanded ME-model analysis capabilities enable genetic recoding, proteostasis, RNA decay, and cofactor essentiality in bacterial models

Santibanez-Palominos, R. A., Tibocha-Bonilla, J. D., +2 authors, Zengler, K.

Abstract: Genome-scale metabolic models (M-models) are widely used to understand and manipulate microbial metabolism in silico, and are characterized by an extensive availability of automated reconstruction, analysis tools, and simulation software. The scope and accuracy of M-models was greatly improved by the development of metabolic and gene expression models (ME-models) aimed to infer the optimal allocation of individual macromolecules such as proteins, RNA, and enzymatic complexes which constrain metabolic fluxes to their availability and abundance. Although ME-models reconstruction and simulation is currently aided by open-source software such as coralME, COBRAme, ECOLIme, and solveME, they lag behind their M-model counterparts and lack formal analysis tools such as gene essentiality analysis, that would facilitate a broader adoption of ME-models in research. Moreover, the rigid and incomplete formulation of ME-models limit advanced analyses they are theoretically capable of, including the simulation of genomically recoded organisms, protein homeostasis, enzyme cofactor essentiality, and RNA decay. Here, we present coralME v1.3, a vastly improved software version aimed to expand the applicability of ME-models by adding support for translation of non-canonical amino acids, protein and RNA degradation reactions, use of arbitrary coupling coefficients and parameter values, and formalized gene and cofactor essentiality analyses. This work expands, formalizes, and streamlines ME-model reconstruction and analyses for applied research.

Paired multiomic profiling defines the healthy human synovium infrapatellar fat pad stromal continuum

Swahn, H., Gomez, I. L., +10 authors, Lotz, M. K.

Abstract: The synovium and infrapatellar fat pad (IPFP) are anatomically contiguous tissues implicated in osteoarthritis, yet their cellular and molecular organization in the healthy human knee remains poorly defined. We combined bulk RNA sequencing with paired single-nucleus transcriptomic and chromatin accessibility profiling to establish a quantitative multiomic reference of healthy human synovium and IPFP. Analysis of 54,716 nuclei from six cadaveric donors identified 23 synovial and 20 IPFP cell populations and indicated that the two tissues share one transcriptionally continuous, largely quiescent stromal compartment. Stromal populations shared core transcriptional programs but differed primarily in extracellular matrix output, with transcripts of inhibitory regulators predominating over those of their functional opponents. TIMP3 exceeded MMP14 in 42 of 43 single-nucleus populations across both tissues and by 13- to 39-fold in bulk tissue. Chromatin accessibility primarily distinguished stromal lineages and resolved transcriptionally defined subtypes, separating synovial lining from sublining fibroblasts and adipocytes from fibroblasts. Fibroblast and adipocyte populations occupied a common transcriptional trajectory accompanied by declining expression of organelle-turnover programs and little proliferation, with no dividing adipocytes and fewer than 0.05% of fibroblasts in the cell cycle. Despite this shared organization, the IPFP exhibited greater inferred intercellular signaling, attributable in part to BMP5, which was not detected in synovium. These findings describe healthy synovium and IPFP as regionally specialized parts of a transcriptionally continuous stromal compartment and provide a quantitative reference for identifying cellular and regulatory changes in joint disease.

Revealing the transcriptomic organization of extrasomatic interfaces using SCOUBI

Aggarwal, B., Jain, S., Sinha, S.

Abstract: Spatial transcriptomics (ST) generates substantial transcript signal outside segmented cell bodies, which is generally discarded by analysis tools. This is especially problematic for brain ST data, as neuronal mRNAs can be localized far from somata - in axons, dendrites, and synaptic compartments - and up to half of the transcripts in ST data may lie outside cell bodies. Here, we present SCOUBI, a computational framework that identifies axon-dendrite interfaces from extrasomatic RNA in ST data, revealing a hitherto overlooked layer of molecular function in neural tissues. It first partitions extrasomatic space into axon- and dendrite-enriched compartments based on known marker genes, using a neural network trained to maximize marker specificity and inter-compartment communication signals. It then defines interfaces as loci where predicted axonal and dendritic compartments converge and exhibit ligand-receptor colocalization. Applied to mouse brain, SCOUBI creates maps of axonal and dendritic compartments and their interfaces that align with known spatial patterns of neurite and synaptic distribution. It identifies transcriptomic differences between interfaces and the surrounding extrasomatic space, revealing region-specific signatures of neuronal signaling and synaptic plasticity. By comparing interface transcript enrichment with local somatic expression, SCOUBI identifies genes that preferentially localize at sites of intercellular communication rather than simply reflecting nearby cell bodies. SCOUBI reveals interface-associated transcripts beyond established synaptic genes, with molecular signatures reproducible across independent samples and imaging platforms. In summary, SCOUBI transforms a substantial and often-discarded aspect of ST data into an interpretable readout of subcellular organization and neural signaling.

ProtCOVER: Family-Aware Conformal Prediction for Variant Effect Predictors on Unseen Protein Families

Fahmy, W., Krikun, E., Alkhateeb, A.

Abstract: Protein language models and related variant effect predictors (VEPs) increasingly classify missense variants in proteins evolutionarily distant from those used for calibration. Split conformal prediction over variants is valid for its own target, a random variant dominated by well-studied families, but deployment needs coverage for a variant of a new family. On ProteinGym clinical substitutions with five zero-shot predictors, Mondrian conformal comes within 0.005 of that target for four predictors, yet family-weighted pathogenic coverage is only 0.823-0.873 at a 0.90 target for all five, without any shift. We present ProtCOVER, a conformal procedure that treats homology families, rather than individual variants, as the exchangeable unit. ProtCOVER (i) calibrates at the family level over label-free homology clusters, giving marginal coverage for a new family under family exchangeability; (ii) adapts to descriptor shift by calibrating conditionally on an estimated density ratio of label-free descriptors (sequence similarity, alignment depth and embedding distance; a variant reweights by the ratio instead), with finite-sample family-weighted coverage, valid as computed, whenever the true ratio is a nonnegative affine function of the estimate; and (iii) can abstain at low effective calibration support, which proved no better than random abstention. Under a shift of its descriptors, ProtCOVER's mean worse-class shortfall below the target is 0.000, against 0.010 for group-weighted and 0.029 for hierarchical conformal prediction, with larger sets (about equal at matched coverage); on orphan families and held-out embedding regions it matches group weighting (0.002 against 0.003). In evaluations following pre-registered protocols, every shift-aware method met the target on new ClinVar variants of benchmark proteins, which ESM-1b separated better; on 12,225 proteins outside the benchmark, every method fell short for pathogenic variants with either of two predictors, ProtCOVER and a variant conditioned on the descriptors the least, within the 0.01 margin of group weighting.

ProtFlap: Structure-based Deep Learning Prediction of Protein Flexibility from Atomic Energy Networks

Lubna, H., Choi, Y.

Abstract: Protein flexibility is a key determinant of molecular recognition, catalysis, and regulation, yet remains difficult to characterise at scale. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations provide detailed descriptions of time-dependent dynamics but are computationally expensive. Although recent AI-based structure prediction methods have substantially improved access to high-quality static protein structures, they inherently lack information regarding the dynamic fluctuations essential for functional understanding. There is therefore a need for scalable, structure-based computational approaches that can infer intrinsic protein flexibility without explicit dynamical sampling. We present ProtFlap, a graph neural network framework for predicting relative residue-level flexibility directly from static protein structures. ProtFlap represents proteins as residue interaction graphs and integrates empirical atomic energy features extracted from a single energy-minimised snapshot with structural and sequence-derived descriptors. Central to our approach is the hypothesis that the local atomic energy network encoded in a frozen protein structure contains sufficient information to estimate structural flexibility. Trained using atomic mobility labels, quantified via normalised root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) derived from short MD simulations, ProtFlap achieves robust and accurate flexibility prediction across diverse protein domains. Feature attribution analysis reveals that van der Waals-related energy terms are the dominant contributors to the prediction.

Evaluating Language Models as Orchestrators in a Tool-First Biomedical Discovery Platform

Sapireddy, V., Chakrabarty, B., +1 author, Roy, A.

Abstract: Large language models (LLMs) increasingly orchestrate agentic biomedical discovery systems, but cloud deployment raises concerns about inference cost, data privacy, and governance. Small language models (SLMs) that run on-premises are an alternative, provided they orchestrate scientific tools about as well as larger models. We built a tool-first, multi-agent system for scientific discovery in which an orchestrator coordinates six specialized tool systems: differential expression, literature retrieval, pathway enrichment, network-based gene prioritization and knowledge-graph analysis, perturbation signature matching, and structural/druggability assessment. In a tool-first design, specialized tools supply the domain knowledge and computation, so the model's main task is coordination, e.g. choosing tools, passing the right parameters, calling them in the right order, and carrying results across turns. With the tools, prompts, workflow, and benchmark held constant, we compared three orchestrators, Gemma 4 (26B, open-weight and locally deployable), Gemini 2.5 Flash, and Claude Sonnet 5. Evaluation used a custom benchmark of 104 single-turn and multi-turn tests, an LLM-as-judge protocol (groundedness and evidence validity), tool-use accuracy and efficiency metrics, and blind expert review. On single-turn tasks, the three models performed comparably, and reviewers did not rate the SLM below the larger models. In multi-turn tasks, Claude Sonnet 5 showed marginally higher evidence validity, Gemma 4 was weakest when parameters had to be assembled across turns, and all three models struggled with tool ordering on memory-dependent tasks. These differences were illustrated with case studies. Finally, we propose strategies to help SLMs address the multi-turn limitations and support cost- and privacy-aware deployment.

A conserved matrix-centred core links fibrosis across human organs and mouse models

Di Carmine, S., Cunningham, R., Mylonas, K. J.

Abstract: Fibrosis develops in many organs through different initiating injuries, yet repeatedly engages inflammation, stromal activation and extracellular-matrix remodelling. Whether these recurring features represent a conserved disease programme, and whether commonly used mouse models reproduce it, remain unresolved. We developed ScarX, an R/Shiny workflow that analyses organ-resolved bulk RNA sequencing, identifies directional overlap across tissues, and ranks human-mouse conserved signals while retaining organ-restricted results. Public RNA-sequencing datasets representing fibrotic or fibrosis-associated disease in human heart, kidney, liver and lung were compared with matched mouse injury models. Differential expression was estimated independently within each organ using DESeq2, followed by cross-organ intersection, functional enrichment, matched-organ effect-profile comparison and a human-first conserved modulation (HCM) score that gives greatest weight to the breadth and magnitude of human disease change, adds support for same-direction mouse effects and penalises discordance. In human disease, 202 genes increased and 35 decreased in the same direction across all four organs. Mouse models contained 409 pan-organ increases and 46 decreases. After gene-symbol harmonisation, 25 gene-direction signatures were conserved across all four organs in both species. These included AEBP1, CDH11, COL1A1, COL1A2, COL3A1, COL5A2, COL6A2, EFEMP2, EMILIN1, LOXL1, LOXL2, TGFB2 and VIM. Matched human-mouse effect profiles varied considerably: Spearman rho was 0.42 for kidney, 0.31 for heart, 0.16 for liver and -0.06 for lung. Despite this uneven gene-level fidelity, 62 same-direction pathways were shared across species, led by extracellular-matrix organisation, collagen fibril organisation, cell-substrate adhesion, wound healing and ECM-receptor interaction. The HCM ranking concentrated structural matrix genes near the top but also identified cell-surface, secreted and regulatory candidates. Among the top 500 candidates, 376 carried at least one local tractability annotation. ScarX therefore separates a compact conserved matrix-remodelling core from a much larger organ- and species-dependent response. The results provide a transparent starting point for target and biomarker selection, while also showing why expression conservation alone cannot establish therapeutic dependence.

Spatial causal discovery reveals novel mechanisms of cellular senescence in human liver

Kummerfeld, E., Wang, Y., +14 authors, Aliferis, C.

Abstract: The complex network of mechanisms that surround cellular senescence, such as its precise causes and effects, are not entirely understood at present. Senescence occurs when a cell permanently ceases dividing, halts apoptosis, stops performing its normal functions, and begins secreting chemicals that can induce other cells in its environment to also become senescent. Senescent cells are present in both healthy and unhealthy tissue, but their abundance is positively correlated with disease and age. A better understanding of senescence could lead to healthier aging, improved recovery from chemotherapy, or increased lifespan. This study used novel machine learning algorithms - with guaranteed properties of correctness under broad assumptions - to investigate senescence in human liver tissue by analyzing Visium spatial transcriptomics data. This produced data-driven models of the causes and effects of senescence, both locally and from nearby tissue. This report includes an assessment of whether the data meet key requirements for causal analysis, the transcripts identified by causal analyses as causes and effects of senescence marker genes, and pathway analyses of those gene sets.

RBM15::MKL1 uncouples SPOC-dependent m6A remodeling from WTAP recruitment

Espinosa, J. A., Chen, M., +6 authors, Krause, D. S.

Abstract: The recurrent t(1;22) translocation associated with pediatric acute megakaryoblastic leukemia generates the RBM15::MKL1 (RM) fusion protein that retains the RNA-binding and SPOC domains of RBM15. RBM15 recruits the N6-methyladenosine (m6A) methyltransferase complex (MTC), of which WTAP is a key regulatory component. How RM engages this machinery and whether the SPOC domain is required for its leukemogenic activity remain unclear. Here, we investigated the molecular determinants of WTAP recruitment by RBM15 and RM and their contribution to RM-mediated disruption of megakaryocyte differentiation. Structure-guided mutagenesis identified a conserved basic surface within the isolated RBM15 SPOC domain that is required for WTAP association. Unexpectedly, disruption of this surface or complete SPOC deletion failed to abolish WTAP association with full-length RBM15 or RM, indicating that additional determinants mediate MTC recruitment. In human erythroleukemia cells, RM impaired TPA-induced megakaryocytic maturation, as assessed by differentiation markers, polyploidization, and morphology; this blockade persisted following SPOC disruption. However, transcriptomic analysis demonstrated that RM lacking the SPOC domain elicited an attenuated gene expression response compared with unmutated RM, including alterations in WNT/Beta-catenin-associated genes. m6A-enriched RNA profiling further revealed extensive remodeling of the m6A epitranscriptome by RM, while SPOC deletion shifted RM-associated m6A changes toward a more wild-type-like state. Together, these findings demonstrate that although the SPOC domain contributes to RM-mediated molecular regulation, it is dispensable for stable MTC association and maintenance of the differentiation blockade, revealing previously unrecognized redundancy in RBM15-mediated recruitment of the m6A machinery.

SLC25A39 loss disrupts mitochondrial redox homeostasis and exposes therapeutic vulnerabilities in glioblastoma

Eales, K. L., Lyu, Y., +10 authors, Tennant, D. A.

Abstract: Glioblastoma is an incurable brain tumour characterised by extensive hypoxia and phenotypic plasticity, both of which drive therapy resistance. Mitochondrial glutathione, dependent on the inner membrane carrier SLC25A39, is central to buffering the oxidative stress that hypoxia generates; its regulation and significance in glioblastoma are nonetheless unknown. We find that SLC25A39 expression is hypoxia-inducible and HIF-dependent, enriched in hypoxic tumour niches and heterogeneously expressed within and between tumours. Its loss depletes mitochondrial glutathione, causing oxidative damage to nucleic acids and lipids, mitochondrial hyperfusion, impaired respiration and a compensatory shift towards glycolysis, glutamine catabolism and serine synthesis. SLC25A39-deficient cells grow poorly, yet resist temozolomide while becoming markedly sensitive to navitoclax, an inhibitor of Bcl-2 and Bcl-xL, which increases cell killing when combined with radiation and temozolomide. In this work, we identify SLC25A39 as a metabolic vulnerability and candidate stratification marker in glioblastoma.

GATA2 SUMOylation Regulates GATA2 Stability to Control Uterine Serous Carcinoma Invasion

Mattix, A. A., Manan-Mejias, P. M., +7 authors, Matson, D. R.

Abstract: Uterine serous carcinoma (USC) is an aggressive uterine cancer subtype that rapidly invades the uterine myometrium and is responsible for 40% of uterine cancer deaths. The molecular mechanisms that underlie this aggressive phenotype are poorly understood. We reported recently that USC tumors with low expression of the zinc-finger transcription factor GATA Binding Protein 2 (GATA2) exhibit aggressive tissue invasion, while retention of GATA2 expression is an independent predictor of 100% recurrence-free and cancer-specific survival in patients with early-stage USC. Here, we explored the mechanism of GATA2 loss in USC tumors that portend invasion. We show that GATA2 in human USC cell lines has a rapid 128-minute protein half-life due to post-translational SUMOylation, which targets GATA2 for ubiquitin-dependent proteasomal degradation. Treatment with small-molecule SUMOylation inhibitors, siRNA-mediated depletion of SUMO-2/3, or mutation of GATA2 K222 and K389 increases GATA2 levels and suppresses invasion in USC cell lines and USC tumor spheroids without impacting cell proliferation or viability. Primary USC tumors from newly diagnosed patients show markedly increased SUMO-2/3 levels and upregulation of SUMOylation pathways compared to other uterine cancer subtypes or benign endometrium, and heat-shock mediated induction of USC SUMO signaling leads to a SUMOylation-dependent reduction in GATA2 levels. Our results implicate SUMOylation as a dynamic regulator of USC GATA2 levels and support a model in which stress-related increases in SUMO activity deplete GATA2 to drive USC invasion. Our findings also raise the possibility that small-molecule SUMOylation inhibitors may benefit patients with early-stage USC disease.

New approach methodologies in nanomedicine: bridging the gap between in silico, in vitro, and in vivo systems.

Radwan Y, Rebolledo LP, +7 authors, Afonin KA.

Abstract: The rise of nanomedicine has led to the regulatory approval of more than 80 nanoparticle (NP)-based therapeutics over the past two decades. Further advancement in the field requires a deeper understanding of the nano-bio interface of NPs, including their uptake, delivery, efficacy, toxicity, and adverse effects. Despite the heavy reliance on conventional cell cultures for NP screening, these models do not recapitulate the in vivo microenvironment, complexity, and physiological responses; hence, follow-up animal studies are required. Nonetheless, because of species-specific differences and ethical concerns around animal studies, developing alternative platforms has become critical. To address these issues, 3D cell culture models such as organoids, spheroids, and organ-on-chip systems have emerged to bridge the gap between in vitro and in vivo systems. Because they better represent cell interactions, gene expression, nutrient gradients, and tissue-specific functions, 3D models also align with the U.S. FDA's promotion of new approach methodologies (NAMs) to reduce animal testing. This review covers examples of organic and inorganic NPs tested in 3D models and discusses organ-specific NAMs, considering their advantages and limitations. The review also highlights how 3D models improve NP evaluation for drug delivery, toxicity, and targeted therapy, and provides a coherent NAMs toolbox for developing in silico methods for NP assessment. Future perspectives are introduced to underscore the importance of integrating in silico NAMs and 3D models to complement in vivo models for advancing precision medicine and improving the translational potential of complex drug products containing nanotechnology.

Label-free Raman spectroscopy for cancer therapy response: from methodological framework to clinical translation.

Alcolea-Rodriguez V, Bresci A, Mühlig A, +4 authors, Vanna R.

Abstract: The early detection of therapy-induced biomolecular changes and the prediction of therapeutic response remain major challenges in oncology. Current clinical methods often assess treatment response through macroscopic or morphological endpoints, whereas molecular alterations associated with sensitivity, resistance, or recurrence may arise earlier and remain undetected. Raman spectroscopy offers a label-free and non-destructive approach to probe the intrinsic biochemical composition of cells, tissues, and biofluids, providing spectral and spatial information that can support therapy response prediction and longitudinal monitoring. This review discusses Raman-based technologies for cancer therapy assessment, from preclinical models to emerging clinical applications. We first outline the clinical need for rapid, non-destructive, and molecularly informative tools, considering tumor heterogeneity, therapy resistance, the tumor microenvironment, and current standards for response evaluation. We then provide a methodological framework for selecting Raman modalities, including spontaneous Raman (SR) spectroscopy, surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS), stimulated Raman spectroscopy (SRS), and coherent anti-Stokes Raman spectroscopy (CARS), together with key considerations on acquisition strategies, multimodal integration, experimental design, preprocessing, feature analysis, and machine learning. We summarize Raman spectral biomarkers associated with therapy-induced changes in nucleic acids, proteins, lipids, metabolites, extracellular matrix components, and anticancer drugs, and critically examine studies using Raman spectroscopy to predict or monitor responses to major therapeutic approaches. Finally, we discuss barriers to clinical translation, including acquisition speed, inter-instrument reproducibility, preclinical model limitations, regulatory requirements, workflow integration, clinical trials, and prospective validation. Overall, Raman spectroscopy emerges as a promising platform for molecular phenotyping in precision oncology, provided that future studies progress toward standardized, clinically validated, and actionable workflows.
