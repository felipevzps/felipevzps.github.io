BioMod: Biological Module Tokenization for Single-Cell Foundation Modeling

Meng, L., Zhu, J., +13 authors, Xu, J.

Abstract: Single-cell foundation models typically represent cells as flat sequences of individual genes, requiring higher-order biological organization to be inferred implicitly from sparse expression profiles. Similar to the evolution from character-level to semantic tokenization in natural language processing,we hypothesize that cellular states can be more efficiently represented using biologically meaningful intermediate units. Here we present BioMod, a 50-million-parameter single-cell foundation model that introduces biological modules as representation units between genes and cells. BioMod constructs an overlapping vocabulary of 660 biological modules derived from curated pathways and data-driven gene programs, replacing thousands of gene-level inputs with compact module representations. Pretrained on approximately 73 million human cells using high-ratio module masking with an asymmetric decoder, BioMod achieves state-of-the-art performance across cell-type annotation, zero-shot batch integration, perturbation-response prediction and drug-response forecasting while maintaining a compact architecture that enables efficient inference on a single GPU. Module-level analyses further demonstrate that BioMod captures biologically meaningful cellular programs and preserves pathway-level organization.

Do LLM Proposers Improve Budgeted Search for Ionizable Lipids?

Singh, H., SINGH, R. K., +2 authors, Gorantla, R.

Abstract: Efficient lipid design under limited experimental budgets is a central challenge in computational chemistry and drug discovery. We introduce LEAF (Lipid Evolutionary Adaptive Framework), a matched-budget protocol for testing whether language-model proposers improve combinatorial molecular search relative to classical baselines. The study uses a 30,044-candidate ionizable-lipid space, a frozen Gaussian-process oracle fitted to experimental potency labels, and a separate active-learning surrogate. Four language models evaluated over eight seeds and three splits show no significant improvement over genetic-algorithm or random-search baselines. For Llama 8B, 79.1% of acquisition-selected queries repeat previously evaluated archive members. Removing duplicates reduces the performance gap on some splits but increases proposal cost and can prevent budget exhaustion. Larger models reduce duplication without establishing superiority or equivalence. Exploratory scored prompts and stricter novelty controls yield positive median differences against the genetic algorithm, yet wide intervals and shared-seed dependence preclude stronger conclusions. A validated single-assay reanalysis preserves the chemotype-cold deficit at a smaller numerical scale, and exploratory expected hypervolume improvement does not reverse the result. Follow-up checks address whether proposers genuinely use injected evidence. These checks include a zero-shot leakage probe, rank-only and chain-of-thought-rationale prompts, a score-shuffle perturbation, an evidence-conflict probe, and a genuine reasoning-mode proposer. They show measurable engagement with supplied information without a corresponding performance gain. LEAF isolates proposal quality as the experimental variable and documents a concrete failure mode of zero-shot LLM proposers under controlled budgets.

Optimizing copy number profiles for tumor cell phylogeny inference from low-coverage single-cell sequencing data

Ahmed, N., Weiner, S., +1 author, Bansal, M. S.

Abstract: Motivation: Accurate reconstruction of tumor cell phylogenies is crucial for understanding cancer evolutionary dynamics, inferring intra-tumor heterogeneity, and designing effective and personalized cancer treatment strategies. One of the most promising and cost-effective approaches for reconstructing tumor phylogenies for large numbers of cells is to use copy number alteration (CNA) data obtained from low-coverage single-cell DNA sequencing of sampled tumor cells. However, error and uncertainty in inferred copy number profiles (CNPs), resulting from low sequence coverage, can have a drastic negative effect on the reconstruction accuracy of tumor cell phylogenies. Results: In this work, we show that careful selection of bin size, a critical tuning parameter for inferring CNPs, is necessary to maximize the accuracy of tumor cell phylogeny inference. Specifically, we (i) use three previously published low-coverage single-cell DNA sequencing datasets from real tumors to demonstrate that even small changes in bin size can have a drastic impact on downstream tumor phylogeny reconstruction, (ii) show that this effect persists even when using simulated low-coverage datasets generated under idealized conditions with minimal noise, and (iii) propose a simple computational approach to select bin sizes that maximize tumor phylogeny accuracy. Our experiments span a wide range of bin sizes and account for the impact of various factors including coverage-level, phylogeny reconstruction method, and CNP inference method, and explicitly account for phylogenetic reconstruction uncertainty. Our evaluation of the proposed computational approach for bin size selection shows that it can significantly improve tumor cell phylogeny reconstruction accuracy compared to baseline methods. Availability: Freely available from https://github.com/Tanveer14/BinSelector.

Efficient approximate Bayesian inference for large, heterogeneous biological datasets

Zanussi, J. T., Miles, C. E.

Abstract: Simulation-based inference (SBI) offers a route to connect biological data with mechanistic models. At the frontier of biology, datasets continue to grow in scale and richness, and increasingly record heterogeneity between individual observations that a mechanistic model can take into account. Approximate Bayesian Computation (ABC) remains a popular approach to SBI due to its ease of use, but incorporating this heterogeneity often requires a separate simulation of each observation, which is prohibitively expensive at scale. A previously proposed technique, minibatch Sequential Monte Carlo ABC (SMC ABC), reduces this cost by simulating a fixed-size random subset of observations for each proposal, at the price of sampling noise that limits precision. Here, we present an adaptive minibatch approach that uses small batches during early exploration and increases the batch size as the posterior is refined. The adaptive batch size is set by the target precision and does not grow with the size of the dataset, so the savings over full-data SMC ABC increase for larger datasets. We demonstrate the method on synthetic biological models and on real single-cell gene-expression data that vary in scale and type of heterogeneity, including cell geometry and initial condition. In each case, the adaptive scheme obtains posteriors close to those of full-data SMC ABC at a fraction of the cost. Altogether, adaptive minibatching improves the tradeoff between speed and precision in SMC ABC and opens the door to mechanistic inference on large, heterogeneous biological datasets.

Surrogate variables and the risk of overfitting in neuropsychiatric gene expression analyses

Hoang, D., Thompson, R., +7 authors, Beckmann, N.

Abstract: The reproducibility crisis in biomedical research calls for re-evaluation of how common analytic approaches affect results. One such approach, surrogate variable analysis (SVA), is widely used to adjust for unmeasured drivers of variation in transcriptomic differential expression analyses. We systematically quantified the impact of SVA using synthetic data with known ground truth and fourteen brain RNA-seq and microarray datasets spanning Alzheimer's disease, autism spectrum disorder, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, and Parkinson's disease. In synthetic data, SVA usage frequently led to high false positive rates. This issue was not revealed by standard diagnostics nor resolved by strategies such as maximizing cross-dataset signal correlation. In real data, using SVA reduced cross-cohort reproducibility of differential expression signatures regardless of the number of surrogate variables included. These findings show that SVA risks inducing overfitting at realistic signal levels, advocating for a cautious, case-by-case application, with a preference for modeling known covariates.

scGSCA: gene-set differential co-expression analysis for single-cell RNA sequencing data

Jin, Z., Irlmeier, R., +3 authors, Ye, F.

Abstract: Understanding disease-associated pathway coordination requires distinguishing phenotype-related co-expression changes from differences across cellular contexts while accounting for cells sampled from the same subject. Bulk gene-set co-expression methods do not directly address the sparse counts, single-cell distributional noise, subject-level dependence, and cell-type heterogeneity of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data. We developed single-cell gene-set co-expression analysis (scGSCA), a framework for pathway-level differential co-expression analysis in scRNA-seq data. scGSCA fits gene-wise zero-inflated negative binomial mixed models with subject-level random effects and computes gene-set co-expression statistics from quantile residual correlations. It provides an overall differential gene-set co-expression analysis and an interaction test to assess whether the co-expression pattern between two cell types varies by sample phenotype. We applied scGSCA to tumor-normal gastric, breast-subtype, and lung-skin comparisons with matched bulk and scRNA-seq data. The overall test identified signals not captured in bulk analysis, including Axon guidance in gastric cancer and estrogen-response, IL6-JAK-STAT3, and TNF/NF-{kappa}B pathways in breast cancer. The interaction test further identified co-expression contrasts across cell types. scGSCA extends gene-set co-expression analysis to single-cell data while retaining subject-level structure and cell-type-specific context. scGSCA is freely available as an open-source R package.

CLARA: Compound Library Active Learning with Reasoning Agents

Srivastava, S. P., Saxena, V., +2 authors, Singh, R. K.

Abstract: Batch active learning for ligand discovery requires a recurring decision about whether to exploit high predicted affinity, reduce model uncertainty, or broaden chemical coverage. We test whether a language model can make this strategy-level decision while candidate ranking and label revelation remain controlled by a deterministic Gaussian-process environment. Across four protein targets, ten paired seeds, and a 360-compound assay budget, a GPT-5.6 Sol agent outperformed random sampling in all 40 matched campaigns. Its final top-2% recall was 0.412 on D2R, 0.374 on Mpro, 0.448 on TYK2, and 0.683 on USP7. It was statistically tied with the strongest fixed policy on Mpro and USP7 but underperformed target-specific leaders on D2R and TYK2. The agent shifted from exploration-oriented early batches toward exploitation and produced 686 testable threshold claims with an 86.3% atomic success rate, although 19.8% of forecasts were not numerically scorable. In 200 prespecified information-ablation campaigns, removing uncertainty, diversity, or memory caused only small endpoint changes, while shuffling explanatory evidence reproduced the full allocation path in 39 of 40 paired campaigns. A deterministic 80% availability constraint reduced recall on every target but preserved the target-dependent pattern of wins and losses. These results support language-model strategy control as a feasible and auditable layer.

How Bacterial Growth-Phase Reshapes Synthetic Gene Circuit Function

Lu, E. J., Del Vecchio, D.

Abstract: The development of biological engineering tools have improved the ability to embed novel functions into living organisms. Concurrently, our understanding of the interface between synthetic genetic circuits and their host has accelerated in the past decade, enabling the development of context-aware predictive models. However, long-term circuit function remains difficult to predict when designs operate in field-relevant conditions. Specifically, while synthetic genetic circuits are typically characterized during exponential growth, these must operate for extended periods of time in nutrient-limited conditions and where bacteria enter stationary growth phase. In this work, we construct a mathematical model that captures the dynamics of a genetic circuit as cells transition into stationary growth phase. To achieve this, we construct synthetic genetic circuits with fluorescent reporter output and measure plasmid DNA copy number, mRNA level, protein level, and live cell count over the course of one week in stationary growth phase. We find that transcription rates plummet shortly after entering stationary phase but settle at a basal value that depends on both the promoter and ribosome binding site. Translation rates also decline in stationary phase, but protein accumulation due to lack of cell division can increase protein levels well beyond exponential phase. Our model recapitulates these findings and is capable of predicting the output of new genetic circuits. This model can serve as a tool to determine how a genetic circuit design may change on longer time scales where cells enter stationary growth phase.

Structure-Based Drug Design of Novel Inhibitors Targeting the Thiamine Biosynthesis Pathway in Bacteria

Krishna, N., Ulaganathan, V.

Abstract: Current generation antibiotics are limited by poor target selectivity and are often mismanaged, leading to widespread antimicrobial resistance. The bacterial thiamine biosynthesis pathway offers compelling alternative since it is highly essential for bacterial survival and yet absent in humans. Genetic perturbations within pathway lead to documented auxotrophy and virulence defects, indicating low mutational tolerance within this pathway. Thiamine phosphate synthase is a key bottleneck enzyme performing the crucial role of coupling thiazole and pyrimidine moieties to form thiamine monophosphate. To develop a suitable inhibitor against it, a structure-based drug design pipeline was implemented. High-throughput virtual screening of nearly 1.5 million molecules provided sufficient exploration of chemical space. Further, R-group substitution was utilized for lead optimization. Structure-Activity Relationship analysis upon molecular mutants reveals patterns among R-group interactions to catalytic residues across various scaffolds. Additionally, ADMET screening and validation using molecular dynamics simulations resulted in a potent orally bioavailable broad-spectrum bactericidal molecule.

Probing isn't Causality in Molecular Models

Srivastava, S. P., Krishna, S. C., +1 author, SINGH, R. K.

Abstract: Linear probes establish whether a chemical variable can be recovered from a molecular representation; they do not establish how that variable contributes to subsequent predictions. We replace a concept-associated scalar component at an intermediate block and measure the change through a separately fitted downstream outcome readout. Comparisons with random directions and architecture-matched random encoders distinguish recoverability, intervention selectivity, and a pretraining-associated selectivity advantage. In Uni-Mol, block-12 total-distortion edits predict the observed energy-change sign for 97.6% of held-out pairs, with Spearman $rho=0.787$. Structure-clustered intervals for the primary direction and encoder contrasts exclude zero. A stricter 20-direction sensitivity analysis does not survive correction across six cells. A post-review analysis finds substantial concept--outcome alignment; intervention effects persist after removal of the same-block outcome direction, but this does not establish that subsequent encoder computation is necessary. In MolCLR, pretraining improves dipole recovery by $0.080,R^2$, whereas concept-minus-random sign-accuracy contrasts are $0.029$ and $-0.015$ at the two tested blocks. Their paired uncertainty is unresolved, so they do not establish an absence of selective effects. We propose an operational evidence ladder whose conclusions are conditional on the concept, intervention, control family, and fitted readout.

Understanding cell fate decisions with temporal attention.

Florian Bürger, Martim Dias Gomes, +2 authors, Katarzyna Bozek

Abstract: Understanding non-genetic determinants of cell fate is critical for developing and improving cancer therapies, as genetically identical cells can exhibit divergent outcomes under the same treatment conditions. In this work, we present a deep learning approach for cell fate prediction from raw long-term live-cell recordings of cancer cell populations under chemotherapeutic treatment. Our Transformer model is trained to predict cell fate directly from raw image sequences, without relying on predefined morphological or molecular features. Beyond classification, we introduce a comprehensive explainability framework for interpreting the temporal and morphological cues guiding the model's predictions. We demonstrate that prediction of cell outcomes is possible based on the video only, our model achieves balanced accuracy of 0.94 and an F1-score of 0.93. Attention and masking experiments further indicate that the signal predictive of the cell fate is not uniquely located in the final frames of a cell trajectory, as reliable predictions are possible up to 8 h before the event. Our analysis reveals distinct temporal distribution of predictive information in the mitotic and apoptotic sequences, as well as the role of cell morphology and p53 signaling in determining cell outcomes. Together, these findings demonstrate that attention-based temporal models enable accurate cell fate prediction while providing biologically interpretable insights into non-genetic determinants of cellular decision-making.
