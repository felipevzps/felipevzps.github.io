Integrating disease and protein networks expands genetic support for therapeutic targets

Zhang, W., Xu, J., +6 authors, Wu, C.

Abstract: Human genetics guides therapeutic target selection, but direct genetic support covers few clinically investigated target-indication pairs, each linking a target to a disease. Here we introduce BRIDGE (Biological Relation-Informed Diffusion of Genetic Evidence), which propagates genetic evidence through coupled protein-protein interaction and disease-similarity networks. Each candidate's score is calibrated against scores obtained after randomly substituting similarly connected genes for genes with direct genetic support. In a retrospective benchmark of 13,022 pairs that had reached at least phase I, BRIDGE supported 572 additional pairs, increasing coverage from 5.1% to 9.5%. Among eligible pairs lacking direct genetic support, 25.3% of network-added pairs had reached launch versus 8.6% of remaining candidates (relative success, 2.94; 95% confidence interval, 2.51-3.45). This enrichment extended to 158 pairs involving 101 genes with no direct genetic support in the benchmark. At a matched selection size of 1,310 pairs, BRIDGE ranking captured 265 launched pairs, compared with 223 under a rule transferring support across neighbouring targets or indications. These findings show that coupling disease relationships with protein connectivity expands the reach of human genetics to additional therapeutic targets and indications while retaining clinical relevance.

GenVinci reconstructs 3D molecular tissue scenes from histology to map senescence across aging and disease

Chen, X., Comiter, C., +30 authors, Shu, J.

Abstract: Spatial omics has generated measurements at unprecedented scale, yet computational approaches remain largely confined to individual modalities and 2D representations. Here we assemble ImageOmicsNet-300M, a corpus of 300 million single-cell and spatial transcriptomic profiles, including 24.9 million H&E-predicted virtual spatial transcriptomics profiles, and 4,623 H&E sections. Of these H&E sections, we generated 1,782 from aging mouse organs and serially sectioned human skin spanning five decades, with matched spatial transcriptomics and snRNA-seq. Building on this resource, we develop GenVinci, a multimodal tissue foundation model that reconstructs 3D tissue scenes from routine 2D histology. GenVinci predicts spatial gene expression from H&E with zero-shot generalization and maps senescence-associated states across 85.8 million cells, revealing cell-type-specific patterns and disease-associated remodeling. Integration with human genetics implicates suprabasal keratinocytes in atopic dermatitis and keratinocyte carcinomas, linked to higher and lower p16 and p21 expression, respectively. These findings establish GenVinci as a framework mapping senescence in 3D across scales.

Benchmarking four large language models on generating and selecting hypotheses that match published findings in 50 studies of ageing biology

Poteshkin, Y., Udodov, E., Batin, M.

Abstract: Objective We asked how often hypotheses generated by large language models for 50 research tasks matched the withheld finding of the published study, and whether model forecasts could identify the matching hypotheses. Methods We built tasks from 50 peer-reviewed studies in ageing biology published after the models' stated training cutoffs; each task gave the background and a research question and withheld the study's finding (the reference finding). GPT-6 Astra, Grok 4.5, Claude Opus 5.5, and Gemini 4 Argon each generated five hypotheses per task by three methods: a single request, a multistep pipeline, and the Omega Point method without literature search (2997 hypotheses). A hypothesis matched the reference finding if at least two of three judge models from other developers scored it at least 3 (primary threshold) or at least 4 (strict threshold) on a 0 to 5 scale; refusals counted as non-matches. A selection rule, chosen from 112 variants evaluated on the Grok 4.5 hypotheses, kept only the hypotheses for which all four predictor models forecast experimental confirmation; the predictors never saw the published finding. The controls were recorded random answers and each generator's first-listed hypothesis. Confidence intervals (95% CIs) were calculated by cluster bootstrap over tasks. Results Of the hypotheses generated by a single request, 13.2% to 18.0% matched the reference finding, compared with 11.2% to 18.8% for the multistep pipeline and 2.8% to 7.6% for the Omega Point method. With a single request, the hypothesis listed first matched in 37.5% of cases (75/200), and the hypotheses in positions 2 to 5 matched in 10.1% (81/800). Among the hypotheses selected unanimously, the match rate was 73.7% (14/19) for Grok 4.5, 47.4% (9/19) for Gemini 4 Argon, and 63.6% (14/22) for Claude Opus 5.5, compared with 12.1% to 17.9% for the random control and 37.5% to 40.0% for the first-listed hypotheses. The selected lists captured 27.3% to 35.0% of all matching hypotheses. Pooled across the three sets, the match rate among selected hypotheses exceeded that among first-listed hypotheses by 23.2 percentage points (pp; 95% CI [13.0, 32.6]). When Gemini 4 Argon re-extracted the reference findings, the match rate among selected hypotheses was 59.6% (34/57), compared with 61.4% (35/57) against the original reference findings for the same tasks. In a manual audit with the generator, judge scores, and selection status hidden, our classifications agreed with the judges' at the primary threshold in 81/100 cases (Cohen's kappa 0.62). Agreement was 89.7% after weighting the sample strata by their size in the corpus. We classified 19 of 30 sampled selected hypotheses as matches, and the judges classified 20 of 30. Conclusions In the evaluated sets, unanimous selection by the four predictors increased the match rate among selected hypotheses; the resulting match rate depended on the matching threshold. Generators listed a matching hypothesis first more often than in later positions, and 50 of the 60 selected hypotheses were listed first. The advantage over the first-listed hypotheses and over the best single predictor at equal list length was statistically significant for the Grok and Opus hypotheses but not for the Gemini hypotheses, and the pairwise differences of the advantage between generators were not statistically significant (p >= 0.16 after Holm correction). We selected the rule using the Grok hypotheses and evaluated it on the Gemini and Opus hypotheses for the same 50 tasks; it has not been tested on new tasks.

Learning spatial cellular state through 3D reconstruction of the cell

Lee, Y. J., Anad, K., +1 author, Lugo-Martinez, J.

Abstract: Subcellular-resolution spatial transcriptomics localizes individual RNA transcripts within cells with high-precision. Existing methods for identifying spatially organized cell populations rely on cellular niches defined by neighboring cells, leaving open whether such populations can be identified from individual-cell representations alone. Moreover, representations that jointly capture transcript identity and three-dimensional (3D) spatial organization have not been systematically learned or biologically evaluated. Here we present a representation learning framework that models each cell as a gene-annotated point cloud of transcripts and trains a rotation-equivariant neural network to reconstruct 3D cell shape, yielding spatially informed transcript embeddings. We further aggregate these embeddings into a cell-state representation that summarizes transcript-level spatial patterns. Our framework outperforms existing point-cloud-based 3D reconstruction methods on cell-shape reconstruction. In MERFISH human uterine cancer, combining the learned embeddings with gene expression improved prediction of CD45RO protein intensity over both gene expression alone and niche-based embeddings. Applied to MERFISH human liver cancer and Xenium human lung datasets, our framework identified distinct spatial patterns between cell populations, including spatially organized subpopulations marked by disease-associated gene expression and patterns associated with pathological tissue structures. Together, these results show that learning transcript representations through 3D cell reconstruction provides a novel approach to capture cell-state heterogeneity and biologically relevant spatial organization in subcellular spatial transcriptomics.

Quantifying Homology and Length Bias in Antimicrobial Peptide Prediction

Paul, B. K., Dip, S. A., +2 authors, Zhang, L.

Abstract: Bacteria are becoming resistant to existing antibiotics faster than new ones are found. Antimicrobial peptides are a promising alternative, but laboratory testing is slow and costly. Candidates are therefore ranked first by a classifier, and published classifiers report near-perfect accuracy. Two controls are missing from the benchmarks behind those numbers. Training and test peptides are not separated by sequence homology. The negative class is not matched to the positive class by length. We propose a controlled evaluation protocol that imposes both, plus a sample-size control that separates matching from the data it removes. We apply it to a four-model ensemble of our own and ten baselines on a benchmark of 16,168 sequences, holding the models fixed. A classifier given nothing but peptide length separates the benchmark far above chance. Homology control costs 0.067 MCC and length matching a further 0.115, taking the reported score from 0.834 to 0.661. Over ten redrawn partitions the length term holds while the homology term shrinks, so length is the effect that survives. The protocol also reorders the methods, and a frozen ESM-2 embedding overtakes the ensemble. Three published benchmarks show the same pattern. We recommend a minimum reporting standard that makes such losses visible.

spicyR enables robust, well-powered co-localisation tests in large spatial omics cohorts

Dohadwalla, S., Rao, S., +3 authors, Patrick, E.

Abstract: Spatial omics technologies can now profile finely resolved cell populations in patient tissue at scale, offering a way to link their arrangement to outcomes. Yet images often differ in size, shape and cell content, and existing tests between patient groups mistake these differences for co-localisation or weight every image equally. We present spicyR v2, which avoids both problems with a fast, permutation-free test that accounts for these differences and weights each image by how precisely it measures co-localisation. In simulations and 74 analyses of 42 public datasets, it controlled false discoveries, had more power than spicyR v1 and was robust to tears, holes and differences in cell number that misled other methods. These analyses also revealed that many studies relating co-localisation to clinical outcomes are underpowered, and spicyR v2 can estimate the number of patients a new study needs. spicyR v2 analyses 100 million cells in minutes, making robust, well-powered tests of co-localisation practical for large spatial omics cohorts.

LipidRT: leveraging retention time coherence for robust lipid annotation in reversed-phase LC-MS-based lipidomics

Regal-Marques, C., Alcoriza-Balaguer, M. I., +7 authors, Lahoz, A.

Abstract: MS/MS data alone is often insufficient for confident lipid identification, while existing retention time (RT)-based methods, despite their promise, have limited applicability. We present LipidRT, an R-based Shiny application that uses RT coherence to validate, refine and expand lipid annotations in untargeted RPLC-HRMS lipidomics in a dataset-specific, software-agnostic manner. By exploiting conserved chromatographic elution relationships as structural constraints and excluding RT-inconsistent annotations from model fitting, LipidRT improves annotation confidence, coverage and consistency while minimizing manual curation.

HARICOT: De Novo Assembly of Raw Nanopore Signals using Fuzzy de Bruijn Graphs

McConnell, U., Ngo, Q., Firtina, C.

Abstract: Nanopore sequencing enables reading long nucleotide sequences by measuring characteristic changes in ionic current as molecules pass through a nanopore. These current measurements, known as raw signals, can be analyzed without translating them into bases (i.e., without basecalling), which provides fast and resource-efficient genome analysis. Prior work, Rawsamble, enables finding overlaps between raw nanopore signals, without basecalling them, and generating assemblies from these overlaps with off-the-shelf assemblers such as miniasm. Constructing assemblies directly from raw signals can substantially reduce redundancy and fragmentation in signal datasets, which is useful for many downstream tasks such as constructing non-redundant and accurate basecalled reads from assembled signals. However, existing overlap-based signal assembly strategies suffer from limited scalability and resolution mainly due to using off-the-shelf assemblers (e.g., miniasm) that are designed for basecalled sequences. While scalability and performance limitations can be addressed by existing de Bruijn graph approaches to assembly, their reliance on exact matches makes these methods too sensitive to the inherently noisy raw nanopore signals. To improve assembly of raw signals using de Bruijn graph approaches, we present HARICOT, a mechanism that constructs assemblies from raw nanopore signals by utilizing fuzzy de Bruijn graphs and Hough-transform-based overlap detection to robustly tolerate errors and noise in raw nanopore signals. Our evaluations on various genomes of different sizes show that HARICOT significantly improves assembly contiguity and quality compared to Rawsamble, helping close the gap to popular assembly tools such as Flye or wtdbg2 operating on nucleotide sequences. At the same time, performance remains comparable to these assemblers, without requiring additional basecalling. We discuss that assembling raw nanopore signals before basecalling enables future work in various ways such as building these de Bruijn graphs in a streaming way and assembly-aware basecalling techniques that can basecall assembly graphs for accurate and redundancy-resolved basecalling. HARICOT is available at https://github.com/STORMgroup/HARICOT . We also provide the scripts to fully reproduce our results on our GitHub page.

SPIDER: Contextualizing Biomedical Knowledge for Evidence-Grounded Scientific Reasoning

Liu, M., Zhao, W., +9 authors, Xie, W.

Abstract: Aggregated relations in biomedical knowledge graphs can obscure evidence and study conditions, complicating the distinction between scientific disagreement and contextual differences. Here we introduce SPIDER, a multi-agent framework grounding biomedical relations in literature evidence and extracting study conditions for interpretation. Applied to a graph integrating 48 databases and ontologies, SPIDER analyzed 1.95 million PubMed abstracts, adding 2.31 million previously unrepresented, literature-supported entity pairs to construct SPIDER-KG. Daily updates incorporate relations and evidence from newly indexed PubMed records. Across 89,937 evidence-supported comparisons of potentially incompatible relations, SPIDER classified 79.0% as contextual differences, and 6.1% as genuine disagreement, achieving 80.3% macro-F1 averaged across both categories against expert annotations. On SPIDER-Bench and four biomedical reasoning benchmarks, SPIDER-KG improved large language models' interpretation of evidence-supported biomedical relationships. Retrospective analysis of newly reported gene--disease relationships further demonstrated how evidence-linked knowledge can contextualize emerging discoveries. SPIDER-KG provides a reusable, incrementally extensible evidence layer for biomedical reasoning.

Breedbase: An Integrated Breeding Informatics System with Statistical Analysis and Decision-Support Tools

Tecle, I. Y., Tantikanjan, T., +18 authors, Mueller, L. A.

Abstract: Breeding programs are becoming increasingly data-intensive, digital and collaborative. One-stop digital breeding ecosystems, in which data storage, field workflow tools, statistical analysis and breeding decision-support tools are seamlessly integrated, are increasingly important. Breedbase (https://breedbase.org) is one of the few open-source platforms that integrate these functionalities within a single system. It provides tools for phenotypic data quality control, descriptive, inferential and predictive statistical analyses. Integrated into the database are tools for single-trial analysis of variance (ANOVA), linear mixed modeling, stability analysis, correlation analysis, principal component analysis, k-means and hierarchical clustering, kinship and inbreeding analysis, genome-wide association study (GWAS), and genomic prediction. Input data and outputs from these analysis tools are visualized in interactive plots and are also available in text files for downloading and sharing. By persisting analysis datasets in the database and applying standardized and automated data preprocessing and analysis workflows, Breedbase is designed to improve efficiency, help maintain data integrity, and support reproducible analyses that can be readily shared among collaborators. Breedbase is open-source software, containerized using Docker, and available for self-hosting or cloud hosting and management by the Mueller lab.

Rac1-Abi-1 Signaling Links DNA Repair to Tumorigenesis

Huang, Y., Xu, J., +22 authors, Zhou, W.

Abstract: The DNA damage response governs genomic integrity, and abnormal DNA repair can drive tumorigenesis. We previously identified a Rac1-Abi-1 signaling pathway in which Rac1 activates protein phosphatase 5, leading to Abi-1-S323 dephosphorylation and enhanced non-homologous end joining (NHEJ). Given the error-prone nature of NHEJ and its potential to promote genomic instability, we sought to determine whether Rac1-Abi-1-mediated regulation of NHEJ contributes to tumorigenesis. Here, we demonstrate the tumorigenic role of Abi-1-S323 dephosphorylation in nonmalignant cell models and multiple tumorigenesis models established using a newly generated genetically engineered Abi-1 mouse model. We first found that the dephospho-mimetic Abi-1-S323A mutant promoted transformation-associated phenotypes in nonmalignant human cells and enhanced orthotopic glioblastoma growth under unperturbed conditions. To further investigate its tumorigenic potential in vivo, we generated CRISPR/Cas9-mediated Abi-1-S296A knock-in mice, in which S296 corresponds to human S323. Abi-1-S296A mouse embryonic fibroblasts exhibited increased proliferation, while mutant mice developed spontaneous tumors involving lymphoid tissues and organs under unperturbed conditions. Furthermore, Abi-1-S296A increased susceptibility to tumorigenesis following DNA damage. In a low-dose whole-body irradiation model, Abi-1-S296A mice showed a trend toward shortened survival and increased tumor development, with lesions including Harderian gland papillary cystadenomas, round-cell neoplasms, and anaplastic sarcomas. In addition, in an azoxymethane/dextran sulfate sodium (AOM/DSS)-induced colorectal tumor model, Abi-1-S296A mice developed significantly more tumors and had a greater tumor burden. These findings identify Abi-1-S323 dephosphorylation as a previously unrecognized regulator of tumorigenesis and establish a new mouse model for investigating the physiological and pathological consequences of constitutive activation of upstream NHEJ-regulatory signaling.

Deep Lineage: Single-cell lineage tracing and fate inference using deep learning.

Mehrshad Sadria, Allen Zhang, +1 author, Gary D Bader

Abstract: Recent advances in single-cell RNA sequencing and lineage tracing techniques have provided valuable insights into the temporal changes in gene expression during development, tumor progression, and disease onset. However, there are few computational methods available to analyze this information to help understand multicellular dynamics. We introduce Deep Lineage, a deep-learning method for analyzing time-series single-cell RNA sequencing with matched lineage tracing data. Our method accurately predicts early cell fate biases and gene expression profiles at different time points within a clone, surpassing existing methods in fate prediction accuracy. We further validate our model using LineageSim, a fate-aware lineage simulator, demonstrating robustness to technical noise such as barcode dropout and clone misidentification. Additionally, we apply Deep Lineage to in silico perturbations in cellular reprogramming and hematopoiesis to identify key genes and pathways associated with cell fate determination and propose testable perturbation hypotheses for experimental follow-up.
